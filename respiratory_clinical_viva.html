Case 1: Fever, Cough and a Focal Area of Bronchial Breathing

Clinical findings

A 46-year-old man has fever, productive cough and right-sided pleuritic chest pain for 4 days. On examination, chest expansion is reduced over the right lower chest. Percussion is dull. Vocal fremitus and vocal resonance are increased, and bronchial breath sounds are heard over the same area.

Q1Localise the abnormality.⌄
Answer: The findings localise to lung parenchyma in the right lower zone, with airless but patent bronchi — a consolidation pattern.
Q2Why are vocal fremitus and vocal resonance increased?⌄
Answer: Sound is transmitted better through solid or fluid-filled lung than through normally aerated lung, provided the bronchus remains patent.
Q3What bedside finding would make pleural effusion more likely instead?⌄
Answer: Markedly reduced or absent breath sounds and vocal resonance with stony dull percussion and reduced chest expansion.

Case 2: Progressive Dyspnoea with Stony Dullness

Clinical findings

A 58-year-old woman has progressive breathlessness and dry cough. The left hemithorax moves less. Trachea is shifted slightly to the right. Percussion is stony dull over the lower two-thirds of the left chest. Breath sounds and vocal resonance are markedly reduced.

Q1What is the physical syndrome?⌄
Answer: A large left pleural effusion.
Q2Why is the trachea shifted away from the affected side?⌄
Answer: A large volume of pleural fluid exerts mass effect and pushes the mediastinum to the opposite side.
Q3What would a tracheal shift toward the affected side suggest instead?⌄
Answer: Volume loss, such as lung collapse or fibrosis.

Case 3: Sudden Dyspnoea with Hyperresonance and Absent Breath Sounds

Clinical findings

A 24-year-old tall man develops sudden pleuritic chest pain and shortness of breath. The right hemithorax is hyperexpanded. Percussion is hyperresonant. Breath sounds and vocal resonance are markedly reduced on the right.

Q1What pleural-space process is most likely?⌄
Answer: Pneumothorax.
Q2Which additional findings would suggest tension physiology?⌄
Answer: Hypotension, tachycardia, severe respiratory distress, distended neck veins and mediastinal/tracheal shift away from the affected side.
Q3What is the immediate management if tension physiology is present?⌄
Answer: Immediate decompression followed by definitive chest tube drainage; treatment should not wait for imaging.

Case 4: Barrel Chest, Quiet Breath Sounds and Prolonged Expiration

Clinical findings

A 68-year-old chronic smoker has slowly progressive exertional dyspnoea. The chest is hyperinflated with reduced bilateral expansion. Percussion is diffusely hyperresonant. Breath sounds are reduced and expiration is prolonged. Heart sounds are distant.

Q1What physiological pattern does this examination suggest?⌄
Answer: Chronic airflow obstruction with hyperinflation, typical of emphysematous COPD.
Q2Why are breath sounds and heart sounds distant?⌄
Answer: Hyperinflated lungs increase the amount of air between the chest wall and the sound source, attenuating transmitted sounds.
Q3Which bedside signs would suggest development of pulmonary hypertension?⌄
Answer: Loud P2, parasternal heave, raised JVP and later signs of right-sided heart failure.

Case 5: Fine End-Inspiratory Crackles with Clubbing

Clinical findings

A 61-year-old man has gradually progressive exertional dyspnoea and dry cough. There is clubbing. Chest expansion is reduced bilaterally. Fine late-inspiratory 'Velcro-like' crackles are heard at both lung bases. There is no wheeze.

Q1What broad pathological process should be suspected?⌄
Answer: Interstitial lung disease with pulmonary fibrosis.
Q2Why is chest expansion reduced?⌄
Answer: Fibrotic, stiff lungs have reduced compliance and reduced inspiratory expansion.
Q3Which bedside cardiovascular sign would suggest advanced disease?⌄
Answer: A loud P2 or right ventricular heave suggesting pulmonary hypertension.

Case 6: Copious Sputum, Clubbing and Coarse Crackles

Clinical findings

A 39-year-old woman has recurrent episodes of cough with large amounts of purulent sputum since adolescence. She has clubbing. Coarse crackles are heard over both lower zones and partly clear after coughing.

Q1What structural airway disorder is suggested?⌄
Answer: Bronchiectasis.
Q2Why may the crackles change after coughing?⌄
Answer: Secretions within dilated bronchi shift or clear after coughing, altering the adventitious sounds.
Q3Which complication should be considered if she develops major haemoptysis?⌄
Answer: Bleeding from hypertrophied bronchial arteries; massive haemoptysis is an emergency.

Case 7: Episodic Wheeze with a Nearly Normal Examination Between Attacks

Clinical findings

A 27-year-old has recurrent episodes of wheeze, chest tightness and nocturnal cough. During an attack there is bilateral polyphonic expiratory wheeze and prolonged expiration. Between attacks the respiratory examination can be normal.

Q1What physiological abnormality is suggested?⌄
Answer: Variable airflow obstruction with bronchial hyperresponsiveness.
Q2What bedside sign during a severe attack would be particularly concerning?⌄
Answer: A silent or very quiet chest, inability to speak full sentences, exhaustion or altered sensorium.
Q3How would you confirm variable airflow obstruction objectively?⌄
Answer: Spirometry showing reversible airflow obstruction or significant variability in peak expiratory flow, depending on the clinical setting.

Case 8: Unilateral Volume Loss with Tracheal Deviation

Clinical findings

A 57-year-old man has progressive cough and breathlessness. The right hemithorax is smaller and moves less. The trachea is deviated to the right. Percussion is dull over the right upper chest and breath sounds are reduced.

Q1What major physiological process is suggested?⌄
Answer: Loss of lung volume on the right, most consistent with lobar or lung collapse.
Q2Why is the trachea pulled toward the affected side?⌄
Answer: Loss of lung volume creates traction on the mediastinum toward the side of collapse.
Q3In an older smoker, what important cause must be excluded?⌄
Answer: An obstructing bronchogenic carcinoma.

Case 9: Amphoric Breathing over a Localised Upper-Chest Lesion

Clinical findings

A 42-year-old man has chronic cough, fever and weight loss. Over the right upper chest there is dull percussion with cavernous or amphoric breath sounds and coarse crackles.

Q1What type of lung lesion can produce amphoric or cavernous breathing?⌄
Answer: A superficial pulmonary cavity that communicates with a patent bronchus.
Q2What are important causes in this clinical setting?⌄
Answer: Tuberculosis, lung abscess, cavitating malignancy and fungal disease.
Q3Which additional clinical feature would strongly support active pulmonary tuberculosis?⌄
Answer: Haemoptysis, evening fever, weight loss, upper-lobe involvement or relevant exposure history.

Case 10: Dullness with a Succussion Splash

Clinical findings

A 35-year-old has fever, breathlessness and pleuritic pain. The affected hemithorax shows reduced expansion. There is an upper area of hyperresonance and a lower area of dullness with a horizontal level clinically suspected. A succussion splash is elicited.

Q1What pleural-space process does this suggest?⌄
Answer: Hydropneumothorax, with both air and fluid in the pleural cavity.
Q2Why is the percussion note different above and below?⌄
Answer: Air collects above and fluid below, creating hyperresonance superiorly and dullness inferiorly.
Q3What important causes should be considered?⌄
Answer: Tuberculosis, bronchopleural fistula, trauma, post-procedural causes and complicated pleural infection.

Case 11: Massive Unilateral Opacity Pattern with Mediastinal Shift

Clinical findings

A 63-year-old man has marked dyspnoea. The left hemithorax is full and moves poorly. Trachea is shifted to the right. Percussion is uniformly very dull over most of the left chest and breath sounds are almost absent.

Q1What bedside syndrome is most likely?⌄
Answer: A massive left pleural effusion.
Q2What alternative process can also cause a nearly opaque hemithorax but shift the mediastinum toward the lesion?⌄
Answer: Complete lung collapse.
Q3What first imaging study is usually sufficient to distinguish these patterns rapidly?⌄
Answer: Chest radiography, often complemented by bedside thoracic ultrasound.

Case 12: Facial Swelling, Dilated Chest Veins and Dyspnoea

Clinical findings

A 66-year-old smoker has facial puffiness, breathlessness and headache. Neck veins are distended. Dilated superficial veins are visible over the upper chest and flow downward. There is no major peripheral oedema.

Q1What vascular syndrome is suggested?⌄
Answer: Superior vena cava obstruction.
Q2Why are superficial chest-wall veins dilated?⌄
Answer: Collateral venous channels enlarge to bypass obstruction of the superior vena cava.
Q3In this age group and clinical context, what cause is most important to exclude?⌄
Answer: Intrathoracic malignancy, especially bronchogenic carcinoma.

Case 13: Shoulder Pain, Hand Weakness and Partial Ptosis

Clinical findings

A 59-year-old smoker has persistent shoulder pain radiating down the medial arm, weakness and wasting of intrinsic hand muscles, mild ptosis and miosis on the same side. Respiratory symptoms are minimal.

Q1Where is the lesion likely located?⌄
Answer: At the lung apex/superior sulcus with involvement of the lower brachial plexus and sympathetic chain.
Q2What explains the ptosis and miosis?⌄
Answer: Interruption of the cervical sympathetic pathway, producing Horner syndrome.
Q3What underlying disease must be suspected?⌄
Answer: An apical bronchogenic carcinoma, classically a Pancoast tumour.

Case 14: Central Cyanosis, Clubbing and a Loud P2

Clinical findings

A 48-year-old with chronic respiratory symptoms has central cyanosis, clubbing, a parasternal heave and loud P2. JVP is mildly elevated. There is no left-sided cardiac murmur.

Q1What major cardiopulmonary complication is suggested?⌄
Answer: Pulmonary hypertension with right ventricular pressure overload.
Q2What does central cyanosis indicate?⌄
Answer: Reduced arterial oxygen saturation or abnormal haemoglobin affecting the tongue and mucous membranes, unlike peripheral cyanosis which is confined mainly to extremities.
Q3What chronic respiratory disorders can lead to this pattern?⌄
Answer: Advanced interstitial lung disease, chronic hypoxic lung disease, bronchiectasis, COPD and chronic thromboembolic disease.

Case 15: Raised JVP, Oedema and Hyperinflated Lungs

Clinical findings

A 70-year-old chronic smoker with longstanding dyspnoea develops ankle swelling and abdominal fullness. JVP is elevated. There is a parasternal heave, loud P2, hepatomegaly and bilateral pitting oedema. The chest is hyperinflated with diffuse rhonchi.

Q1What cardiovascular complication of chronic lung disease is present?⌄
Answer: Cor pulmonale with right-sided heart failure.
Q2Why is the left ventricular apex often not displaced?⌄
Answer: The primary problem is right ventricular pressure overload rather than left ventricular dilatation.
Q3What is the major chronic driver of pulmonary hypertension in this setting?⌄
Answer: Chronic hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction with vascular remodelling.

Case 16: Acute Dyspnoea with Clear Lungs and Tachycardia

Clinical findings

A 54-year-old woman develops sudden breathlessness and pleuritic chest pain 5 days after major surgery. Pulse is 118/min, oxygen saturation is reduced, but lung auscultation is largely clear. There is mild unilateral calf swelling.

Q1What diagnosis should be considered urgently?⌄
Answer: Pulmonary embolism.
Q2Why can the chest examination be nearly normal?⌄
Answer: The primary problem is pulmonary vascular obstruction rather than a large airway or alveolar process.
Q3Which bedside finding would suggest haemodynamic compromise?⌄
Answer: Hypotension, syncope, raised JVP or signs of acute right ventricular strain.

Case 17: Upper-Lobe Fibrotic Signs with Volume Loss

Clinical findings

A 50-year-old man with previous pulmonary infection has chronic cough and exertional dyspnoea. The upper chest on one side is flattened with reduced expansion. Trachea is pulled toward that side. Percussion is dull and bronchial breathing is heard over the upper zone.

Q1What chronic structural process is suggested?⌄
Answer: Upper-lobe fibrosis with volume loss.
Q2Why is the trachea pulled toward the affected side?⌄
Answer: Fibrotic contraction reduces lung volume and exerts traction on the mediastinum.
Q3Which previous disease is a common cause in endemic settings?⌄
Answer: Pulmonary tuberculosis.

Case 18: Unilateral Dullness with Preserved or Increased Voice Transmission

Clinical findings

A patient has fever and cough. Percussion over the right lower zone is dull. Breath sounds are bronchial and vocal resonance is increased. There is no major tracheal shift.

Q1Which physical sign most strongly argues against a simple pleural effusion?⌄
Answer: Increased rather than decreased vocal resonance.
Q2What does bronchial breathing imply anatomically?⌄
Answer: A patent bronchus transmitting high-frequency sounds through surrounding airless lung.
Q3What additional auscultatory voice sign may be present?⌄
Answer: Bronchophony, egophony or whispered pectoriloquy.

Case 19: Reduced Breath Sounds after Thoracic Surgery

Clinical findings

A 62-year-old man develops dyspnoea after upper abdominal surgery. The right lower chest moves less. Percussion is dull, breath sounds are reduced, and there is no major pleuritic pain. Oxygen saturation is mildly low.

Q1What common postoperative process should be considered?⌄
Answer: Basal atelectasis or lobar collapse.
Q2What bedside factor helps distinguish collapse from a sizeable pleural effusion?⌄
Answer: Mediastinal or tracheal shift toward the affected side suggests volume loss; a large effusion tends to push it away.
Q3What simple preventive measures are important in postoperative patients?⌄
Answer: Early mobilisation, adequate analgesia, deep-breathing exercises/incentive spirometry where appropriate, and treatment of retained secretions.

Case 20: Paradoxical Abdominal Movement and Orthopnoea

Clinical findings

A 56-year-old with neuromuscular weakness reports breathlessness that is much worse when lying flat. There is reduced chest expansion and paradoxical inward movement of the abdomen during inspiration when supine. Breath sounds are otherwise clear.

Q1What respiratory muscle problem is suggested?⌄
Answer: Diaphragmatic weakness, possibly bilateral.
Q2Why is dyspnoea worse when supine?⌄
Answer: In the supine position, abdominal contents push the weakened diaphragm cephalad and reduce inspiratory capacity further.
Q3Which bedside or physiological tests would help confirm respiratory muscle weakness?⌄
Answer: Supine versus upright vital capacity, maximal inspiratory pressure, sniff nasal inspiratory pressure, diaphragmatic ultrasound or fluoroscopic sniff testing depending on availability.
