Clinical Case Summary & Differential Diagnosis (History Phase)

Case Summary (Positive Findings):

  • Patient Profile: 72-year-old female housewife and farm worker, postmenopausal, with a significant history of biomass fuel exposure (45-50 years), resident of Ghaziabad.
  • Respiratory & Constitutional Symptoms: Chronic progressive exertional dyspnea (mMRC Grade III) for 1.5 years, accompanied by a recent onset of low-grade intermittent fever with evening rise for 1 week, dry persistent cough for 1 week, marked anorexia, and significant weight loss (5-10 kg).
  • Past Medical History: Known case of Congestive Heart Failure (diagnosed 1 year ago with EF ~20% following hospitalization for dyspnea and pedal edema), on Met XL, Dytor, and Dapagliflozin. Also on Gabapentin NT. Cholecystectomy 2 years ago.

Probability-Based Differential Diagnoses (on the basis of History):

  1. Pulmonary Tuberculosis (~35%): In an elderly Indian female with low-grade fever showing evening rise, dry cough, anorexia, and marked weight loss, pulmonary tuberculosis is the primary suspect. Chronic biomass exposure increases susceptibility to chronic lung infections.
  2. Decompensated Heart Failure (HFrEF) secondary to Ischemic or Dilated Cardiomyopathy (~25%): Patient has a established diagnosis of heart failure with severely reduced EF (20%) on guideline-directed medical therapy. Subacute worsening of dyspnea and cough could represent decompensation triggered by infection or medication non-compliance.
  3. Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) or Biomass-Induced Lung Disease (~18%): 45-50 years of biomass smoke exposure (cooking with chulha) and farm work, presenting with chronic progressive exertional dyspnea and dry cough, highly suggestive of biomass-induced chronic airway disease.
  4. Community-Acquired Pneumonia (CAP) on a background of HFrEF (~12%): Acute onset of fever and cough for 1 week with worsening dyspnea in an elderly patient, which may also precipitate cardiac decompensation.
  5. Bronchogenic Carcinoma (~10%): Elderly female presenting with chronic progressive dyspnea, dry cough, significant weight loss, anorexia, and long-standing exposure to biomass smoke (a known carcinogen).

[PHASE 1 — HISTORY PRESENTATION

]Q1 You stated that the dyspnoea is continuous but aggravated by exertion. Is she breathless at rest, and what exact activity currently produces breathlessness?

MODEL_ANS1 The present functional limitation should be described concretely—for example, breathlessness while walking on level ground, after a specified distance, while dressing, or at rest. The current mMRC grade should be stated separately from the grade at symptom onset; simply writing “Grade 0 progressed to Grade III” is insufficient without describing the corresponding activity limitation. Kundu recommends analysing dyspnoea by onset, progression, precipitating activity and associated symptoms; Alagappan describes cardiovascular limitation using functional classes.

Q2 Does she have orthopnoea? How many pillows does she use, and how rapidly does breathlessness appear after lying flat?

MODEL_ANS2 Orthopnoea is breathlessness in the recumbent position relieved by sitting upright or elevating the head. It should be quantified by the number of pillows and the time taken for symptoms to appear and resolve. This is especially important in a patient with a documented ejection fraction of approximately 20%.

Q3 Has she ever awakened from sleep with sudden breathlessness, choking, sweating or a need to sit near an open window?

MODEL_ANS3 These features should be sought to establish paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea. PND generally awakens the patient after a period of sleep and is relieved gradually after assuming an upright posture; it is an important symptom of left-sided cardiac decompensation.

Q4 Are chest pain, palpitations, dizziness, presyncope or syncope associated with the dyspnoea?

MODEL_ANS4 These symptoms should be specifically documented because they may suggest ischaemic heart disease, arrhythmia, valvular disease or reduced cardiac output as the cause or precipitant of heart failure. Their absence should be written as relevant cardiovascular negatives rather than omitted.

Q5 Has she developed pedal oedema again, facial puffiness, abdominal distension, right upper abdominal discomfort, reduced urine output or nocturia?

MODEL_ANS5 The history should assess systemic venous congestion and renal consequences of heart failure. Relevant symptoms include recurrent dependent oedema, abdominal distension, hepatic discomfort, oliguria and nocturia. Exertional dyspnoea, nocturnal cough and extremity oedema are recognised clinical features supporting heart failure.

Q6 Was the cough completely dry throughout, or was there any sputum, haemoptysis, wheeze, chest tightness or pleuritic chest pain?

MODEL_ANS6 Cough should be analysed by duration, dry or productive character, sputum, haemoptysis, diurnal or postural variation, triggers and associated wheeze, chest pain or breathlessness. Haemoptysis, pyrexia, weight loss and dyspnoea accompanying cough are alarm features.

Q7 Is the cough worse at night, in the early morning, after lying down, or after exposure to smoke, dust or cold air?

MODEL_ANS7 Nocturnal cough may occur in left ventricular failure, asthma, chronic bronchitis, aspiration and postnasal drip. Morning cough suggests chronic bronchitis or bronchiectasis, while cough after lying down may suggest cardiac failure or gastro-oesophageal reflux. These distinctions help separate cardiac from respiratory causes.

Q8 Was the fever objectively documented? What was the maximum temperature, frequency of spikes and relationship to the evening?

MODEL_ANS8 Fever should be documented by measured temperature, duration, pattern and daily variation. An intermittent fever returns to baseline between spikes, whereas a remittent fever fluctuates but does not return to baseline. “Low-grade” and “evening rise” should ideally be supported by recorded temperatures.

Q9 Apart from chills and rash, was the fever associated with night sweats, sore throat, sputum, dysuria, diarrhoea, altered sensorium or localising pain?

MODEL_ANS9 A systematic search for the infective focus is essential. In this case, infection may represent a respiratory illness or a precipitating factor for decompensation of pre-existing heart failure. Infection is specifically recognised as a precipitant of cardiac failure.

Q10 Did the breathlessness worsen abruptly during the week in which fever and cough appeared?

MODEL_ANS10 The chronology should clearly separate the chronic illness from the recent acute event. An ideal history would state whether the patient had stable chronic exertional dyspnoea for 1.5 years followed by acute worsening with fever and cough, suggesting an infective respiratory episode precipitating cardiac or obstructive-airway decompensation.

Q11 Over what period did she lose 5–10 kg, and was the weight measured? Does she have night sweats, tuberculosis contact, haemoptysis or persistent chest pain?

MODEL_ANS11 Weight loss should be quantified using previous and current measured weights and linked to a precise duration. Because weight loss with reduced appetite may accompany tuberculosis, malignancy or chronic infection, the history must include night sweats, haemoptysis, tuberculosis exposure and persistent focal symptoms. COPD itself may also be associated with muscle wasting and weight loss, but this should not be assumed without excluding other causes.

Q12 What was the documented cause of the previous heart failure—coronary disease, hypertension, valvular disease or cardiomyopathy?

MODEL_ANS12 “Heart failure with EF 20%” describes severity of systolic dysfunction but not its aetiology. The previous echocardiogram, ECG, coronary evaluation, blood-pressure history, valve findings and discharge diagnosis should be reviewed. Heart failure may result from ischaemic, hypertensive, valvular or cardiomyopathic disease.

Q13 Has she previously been diagnosed with hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease, anaemia, thyroid disease, asthma, COPD or tuberculosis?

MODEL_ANS13 These comorbidities must be asked directly because they can cause dyspnoea, contribute to cardiac dysfunction or alter treatment. Severe anaemia is a recognised non-cardiopulmonary cause of dyspnoea, while COPD commonly coexists with heart failure.

Q14 Give the exact generic names, strengths, frequency and adherence for every medicine, including over-the-counter drugs. Has she missed her diuretic or increased salt or fluid intake?

MODEL_ANS14 Drug history should record the generic name, dose, route, frequency, indication, duration, adherence and adverse effects. It should also establish whether the diuretic was taken regularly or “as required,” and whether recent dietary or medication non-adherence could have precipitated congestion. The strength of “Met XL” must not be guessed from an unclear prescription. Medical and dietary noncompliance are recognised precipitating factors for heart failure.

Q15 How was the biomass exposure quantified—fuel type, hours per day, years of exposure, kitchen ventilation—and was there additional farm-dust, pesticide, passive-smoking or animal exposure?

MODEL_ANS15 Indoor smoke from cooking or heating and occupational exposure to organic or inorganic dust are recognised COPD risk factors. Exposure should therefore be quantified by fuel type, daily duration, total years, ventilation and concurrent agricultural exposures. A non-smoking elderly woman may still have clinically important obstructive-airway disease from prolonged biomass exposure.

]Q1 What are the key differential diagnoses for a 72-year-old female presenting with chronic progressive shortness of breath, recently complicated by a 1-week history of fever and cough?
MODEL_ANS1 Differentials include an acute respiratory infection or exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)/Cor Pulmonale (given the 45–50 years of biomass exposure), pulmonary tuberculosis (given the evening rise of temperature and weight loss), and decompensated heart failure with a superimposed respiratory infection (given the history of EF 20% and previous pedal edema). (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Respiratory System; Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Part I, Respiratory System).

Q2 The patient is on Tablet Met XL, Dytor, and Dapa. What are the generic names and roles of these medications in a patient with Heart Failure with reduced Ejection Fraction (HFrEF)?
MODEL_ANS2 Met XL is Metoprolol succinate (a cardioselective beta-blocker that reduces mortality), Dytor is Torsemide (a loop diuretic for symptomatic relief of fluid overload/pedal edema), and Dapa is Dapagliflozin (an SGLT2 inhibitor which reduces heart failure hospitalizations and mortality). (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Cardiovascular System).

Q3 The patient has a significant history of biomass fuel exposure for 45–50 years. How does this contribute to her clinical presentation?
MODEL_ANS3 Prolonged indoor biomass fuel exposure (such as chulha smoke) is a major risk factor for developing COPD and subsequent Cor Pulmonale (right heart failure secondary to pulmonary hypertension), independent of cigarette smoking. This strongly correlates with her chronic progressive dyspnea and cough. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Respiratory System, COPD).

Q4 The history notes an evening rise of temperature, significant weight loss (5–10 kg), and a dry cough. What specific infectious etiology must be strongly suspected in the Indian clinical setting?
MODEL_ANS4 Tuberculosis must be strongly suspected in any patient presenting with chronic cough, evening rise of temperature, anorexia, and significant weight loss in an endemic region. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Part I, Respiratory System).

Q5 You mentioned the orthopnea/PND status was unclear. Why is it absolutely critical to establish the presence or absence of Paroxysmal Nocturnal Dyspnea (PND) and orthopnea in this specific patient?
MODEL_ANS5 PND and orthopnea are hallmark symptoms of left ventricular failure (pulmonary venous congestion). Given her past diagnosis of HFrEF (EF 20%), differentiating orthopnea from the positional dyspnea of severe COPD/airway disease is vital for assessing her current volume status and cardiac compensation. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Part I, Cardiovascular System).

Q6 The patient helps in farming. What specific occupational respiratory hazards or conditions might she be exposed to?
MODEL_ANS6 Farming exposes patients to organic dust, molds (which can cause Hypersensitivity Pneumonitis/Farmer's lung), pollens, and animal dander, which can exacerbate underlying airways disease or cause restrictive lung diseases. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Respiratory System).

Q7 The patient is taking Tab Gabapin NT. What does this medication contain and what might it indicate about her unmentioned past medical history?
MODEL_ANS7 Gabapin NT typically contains Gabapentin and Nortriptyline. It is heavily utilized for neuropathic pain. Its presence indicates a missing history of a neuropathic condition, such as diabetic neuropathy, post-herpetic neuralgia, or severe osteoarthritis/radiculopathy. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Nervous System).

Q8 The patient was diagnosed with an EF of 20% a year ago. What key historical points should you ask to determine the underlying etiology of her HFrEF?
MODEL_ANS8 You must ask about a history of ischemic heart disease (anginal chest pain, past myocardial infarctions), long-standing hypertension, diabetes mellitus, palpitations/arrhythmias, or prior viral illness suggestive of myocarditis. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Part I, Cardiovascular System).

Q9 The cough is described as 'dry'. How does the character of the cough help differentiate between her possible underlying diseases?
MODEL_ANS9 A dry cough may be seen in early tuberculosis, interstitial lung disease, or as a symptom of pulmonary venous congestion in heart failure (cardiac asthma). A chronic productive cough with copious morning expectoration would point more heavily towards chronic bronchitis/COPD. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Respiratory System - Symptoms).

Q10 You used the mMRC grade to describe her dyspnea. For a patient with a known EF of 20%, what alternative dyspnea classification scale should be explicitly documented?
MODEL_ANS10 The New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification must be documented to assess the severity and functional limitation of her heart failure. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Part I, Cardiovascular System).

Q11 The patient had a cholecystectomy 2 years ago. Is this relevant to her current presentation, and if so, how might it connect to severe right heart failure?
MODEL_ANS11 While usually an independent event, severe right heart failure can cause congestive hepatomegaly and stretching of the liver capsule, causing right upper quadrant pain. This is occasionally mistaken for biliary colic prior to proper ultrasound and clinical evaluation. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Part I, Abdomen).

Q12 You mentioned the fever has no chills, rigors, or rashes. What other important negative history should you elicit in a patient presenting with an intermittent fever in this region?
MODEL_ANS12 History of burning micturition (UTI), altered sensorium, joint pains, bleeding manifestations, and history of travel or mosquito bites (malaria, dengue) should be elicited to rule out common tropical fevers. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Part II, Fever).

Q13 Significant weight loss and anorexia are present. Given her age and history, apart from Tuberculosis, what are the other major differentials for her weight loss?
MODEL_ANS13 Important differentials include an underlying malignancy (especially lung cancer given chronic biomass exposure), severe Cardiac Cachexia (due to end-stage HFrEF), and uncontrolled diabetes mellitus. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, General Examination).

Q14 You mentioned over-the-counter (OTC) medications for shortness of breath. Why is this detail highly concerning in a patient with heart failure?
MODEL_ANS14 OTC medications might include NSAIDs (which cause fluid retention and worsen heart failure), unrecognized bronchodilators that can cause tachycardia, or alternative medicines that can interact with her prescribed Beta-blockers or Diuretics. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Cardiovascular System).

Q15 What critical cardiovascular symptoms did you fail to explicitly mention in your relevant negative history for this patient?
MODEL_ANS15 You missed explicitly documenting the presence or absence of chest pain (angina), palpitations, syncope, or claudication, which are vital for a patient with established severe left ventricular dysfunction to rule out active ischemia or arrhythmias. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Part I, Cardiovascular System).

[PHASE 2 — PHYSICAL EXAMINATION PRESENTATION

]Q1 Which two essential observations are missing from the vital signs in this febrile, breathless patient?

MODEL_ANS1 Body temperature and oxygen saturation by pulse oximetry are missing. Temperature is essential because fever is an active complaint, while SpO₂ helps assess hypoxaemia even when cyanosis, tachypnoea and accessory-muscle use are absent. Respiratory assessment should include rate, rhythm, depth, pattern and evidence of respiratory distress. Kundu, General Survey—Respiration; Alagappan, Respiratory System—Symptoms, Signs and General Examination.

Q2 Is the description of the pulse complete?

MODEL_ANS2 No. In addition to rate and rhythm, the pulse should be assessed for volume, character, tension, vessel-wall condition, equality and peripheral perfusion. In severe left ventricular systolic dysfunction, one should particularly look for a low-volume pulse, pulsus alternans, cold extremities and delayed capillary refill. The absence of radio-radial and radio-femoral delay was appropriately documented.

Q3 How do you interpret a JVP recorded as “8 cm H₂O”?

MODEL_ANS3 The reference point must be stated. If 8 cm H₂O represents total pressure above the right atrium, it is within the textbook normal limit of approximately 9 cm H₂O. If it means 8 cm above the sternal angle, it is markedly raised because the normal vertical height above the sternal angle is approximately 3–4 cm in Kundu and 4–5 cm in Alagappan. The patient’s position, vein examined and waveform should also be stated.

Q4 Which jugular vein should preferably be examined, and how?

MODEL_ANS4 The right internal jugular vein is preferred because it is in direct continuity with the right atrium. The patient is positioned at approximately 45°, the neck is relaxed, tangential illumination is used, and the upper level of venous pulsation is measured vertically from the sternal angle. Abdominojugular reflux should be assessed when heart failure is suspected.

Q5 Was the lymph-node examination adequate?

MODEL_ANS5 No. Recording only the absence of axillary lymphadenopathy is insufficient. In an elderly patient with cough, fever, weight loss and an upper-zone lesion, one should examine cervical, supraclavicular, scalene and axillary nodes, documenting site, size, consistency, mobility, tenderness and matting. Alagappan specifically includes scalene and significant lymph nodes in respiratory examination.

Q6 How do you interpret the chest dimensions of transverse 31 cm and anteroposterior 23 cm?

MODEL_ANS6 The transverse-to-AP ratio is approximately 31:23 or 1.35:1, close to the normal textbook ratio of 7:5 or 1.4:1. This supports an elliptical chest and does not constitute a barrel chest. In a barrel chest, the AP and transverse diameters approach 1:1.

Q7 How should the total chest expansion of 4 cm be interpreted?

MODEL_ANS7 It is mildly reduced according to Alagappan, which gives a normal expansion of approximately 5–8 cm. However, total expansion alone conceals the important asymmetry: the right hemithorax expanded only 1 cm compared with 3 cm on the left. Expansion should be measured below the breasts in women, and identical areas must be compared.

Q8 What is the clinical significance of diminished movement in the right supraclavicular, infraclavicular and upper posterior regions?

MODEL_ANS8 It localises the abnormality predominantly to the right upper zone. Unilateral restriction may occur in consolidation, collapse, fibrosis, pleural disease or pneumothorax. The upper lobes are assessed mainly from the front, although the posterior segment is accessible posteriorly.

MODEL_ANS9 Consolidated lung becomes relatively solid and therefore transmits laryngeal vibrations to the chest wall more effectively, provided the supplying bronchus remains patent. Q10 What does a dull percussion note over the same area indicate?

MODEL_ANS10 Dullness indicates loss of the normal air content of the underlying lung. When it occurs with reduced movement and increased vocal fremitus, it supports parenchymal consolidation. MODEL_ANS11 One should seek a bronchial or tubular breath sound, particularly high-pitched bronchial breathing, over the area of dullness. MODEL_ANS12 Vocal resonance should be compared over corresponding areas. Expected abnormalities in consolidation include bronchophony, whispered pectoriloquy and aegophony. Q13 How would you distinguish this finding from a pleural effusion?

MODEL_ANS13 Pleural effusion usually produces diminished movement, decreased vocal fremitus, stony dullness, diminished or absent breath sounds and decreased vocal resonance. Q14 How would you distinguish consolidation from collapse or fibrosis?

MODEL_ANS14 Consolidation with a patent bronchus generally produces increased fremitus, dullness, bronchial breathing and increased vocal resonance, usually without major mediastinal displacement. Collapse and fibrosis tend to cause volume loss, with tracheal and mediastinal displacement towards the affected side. Collapse with an obstructed bronchus usually has reduced fremitus and reduced breath sounds.

Q2 You documented the Jugular Venous Pressure (JVP) as approximately 8 cm H₂O. How is JVP correctly measured at the bedside, and is 8 cm H₂O considered normal or elevated?
MODEL_ANS2 JVP is measured by determining the vertical height of the oscillating top of the internal jugular venous column above the sternal angle, with the patient reclining at 45°. The right atrium is located 5 cm below the sternal angle. A normal JVP is up to 3–4 cm above the sternal angle (i.e., 8–9 cm H₂O from the right atrium). Therefore, 8 cm H₂O (if measured from the right atrium) is at the upper limit of normal. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Part III, Measurement of Venous Pressure).
