Case 1: Sudden Right-Sided Weakness with Language Difficulty

Clinical findings

A 64-year-old right-handed man develops sudden weakness of the right face and limbs. He understands simple commands poorly, produces little meaningful speech, and cannot repeat a short sentence. Right upper limb weakness is greater than lower limb weakness. Plantar response is extensor on the right.

Q1Localise the lesion.⌄
Answer: Dominant left cerebral hemisphere, most likely the left middle cerebral artery territory involving frontal-parietal language and motor regions.
Q2What type of language disorder is suggested?⌄
Answer: Aphasia with impaired comprehension and repetition; the exact subtype depends on fluency and naming, but a large dominant perisylvian lesion should be suspected.
Q3Which bedside finding would make a cortical rather than capsular lesion more likely?⌄
Answer: Cortical signs such as aphasia, neglect, gaze preference, cortical sensory loss, apraxia, or visual field defect.

Case 2: Dense Hemiparesis without Cortical Signs

Clinical findings

A 58-year-old hypertensive man develops sudden weakness of the left face, arm and leg. Power is 2/5 throughout the left side. Sensation is intact. There is no aphasia, neglect, visual field defect or gaze deviation.

Q1Where is the lesion most likely?⌄
Answer: A compact motor pathway lesion, classically the posterior limb of the right internal capsule or basis pontis.
Q2Why are face, arm and leg affected together?⌄
Answer: Corticobulbar and corticospinal fibres are densely packed in the internal capsule, so a small lesion can produce proportionately dense weakness.
Q3What vascular mechanism is classically associated with this pattern?⌄
Answer: A lacunar infarct due to small-vessel lipohyalinosis, often related to chronic hypertension or diabetes.

Case 3: Ipsilateral Facial Weakness with Contralateral Limb Weakness

Clinical findings

A 52-year-old man has sudden diplopia and weakness of the right arm and leg. Examination shows left lower motor neuron facial weakness and right-sided pyramidal weakness. Sensory examination is largely normal.

Q1Localise the lesion.⌄
Answer: Left pons, where the facial nerve fascicle/nucleus and corticospinal tract lie close together.
Q2Why is the facial weakness ipsilateral but limb weakness contralateral?⌄
Answer: Cranial nerve fascicles affect the same side of the face before exiting, while corticospinal fibres cross lower down at the medullary pyramids.
Q3What bedside pattern is this an example of?⌄
Answer: A crossed or alternating brainstem syndrome.

Case 4: Hoarseness, Dysphagia and Contralateral Pain Loss

Clinical findings

A 60-year-old develops sudden vertigo, vomiting, hoarseness and difficulty swallowing. There is reduced pain and temperature sensation over the left face and right side of the body, left-sided limb ataxia and left Horner syndrome. Power is preserved.

Q1Localise the lesion.⌄
Answer: Left lateral medulla.
Q2Which structures explain the dysphagia and hoarseness?⌄
Answer: Nucleus ambiguus and its fibres involving cranial nerves IX and X.
Q3Why is motor power relatively preserved?⌄
Answer: The corticospinal tract lies ventromedially and is usually spared in a lateral medullary lesion.

Case 5: Diplopia with Ptosis and a Dilated Pupil

Clinical findings

A 49-year-old woman develops sudden severe headache followed by ptosis and diplopia. The affected eye rests 'down and out'. The pupil on that side is dilated and poorly reactive.

Q1Which cranial nerve is affected?⌄
Answer: The oculomotor nerve, cranial nerve III.
Q2Why is pupillary involvement clinically important?⌄
Answer: Parasympathetic pupillary fibres lie superficially in the nerve and are vulnerable to external compression; a painful pupil-involving third nerve palsy raises concern for a compressive aneurysm.
Q3What diagnosis must be urgently excluded?⌄
Answer: A posterior communicating artery aneurysm or other compressive intracranial lesion.

Case 6: Horizontal Diplopia Worse on Looking to One Side

Clinical findings

A 46-year-old man reports horizontal diplopia that worsens when looking to the right. The right eye cannot abduct fully. Other ocular movements and pupils are normal.

Q1Which nerve is affected?⌄
Answer: Right abducens nerve, cranial nerve VI.
Q2Why can this nerve be affected by raised intracranial pressure?⌄
Answer: Its long intracranial course and tethering make it vulnerable to stretch, producing a false-localising sixth nerve palsy.
Q3What additional examination findings would you actively seek if raised intracranial pressure is suspected?⌄
Answer: Papilloedema, headache pattern, vomiting, altered sensorium, and other focal neurological signs.

Case 7: Fluctuating Ptosis and Fatigable Weakness

Clinical findings

A 32-year-old woman has intermittent diplopia and ptosis, worse in the evening. Limb power is near normal initially but falls with repeated testing. Reflexes and sensation are normal. There is no sphincter involvement.

Q1Which level of the motor system is most likely affected?⌄
Answer: The neuromuscular junction.
Q2What bedside feature is most useful here?⌄
Answer: Fatigability: weakness worsens with repeated or sustained activity and improves with rest.
Q3What important bedside safety assessment should be performed?⌄
Answer: Assess bulbar and respiratory involvement, including swallowing, counting/speech, neck flexion, and forced vital capacity where available.

Case 8: Ascending Weakness with Areflexia

Clinical findings

A 28-year-old man develops tingling in both feet followed over four days by progressive leg and then arm weakness. Reflexes are absent. Plantars are flexor. Sensory loss is mild. There is no clear sensory level.

Q1Where is the lesion localised?⌄
Answer: Peripheral nerves/nerve roots, producing an acute polyradiculoneuropathy.
Q2Which bedside finding argues strongly against an upper motor neuron lesion?⌄
Answer: Generalised areflexia without spasticity or extensor plantars.
Q3What complication must be monitored even if limb weakness appears modest?⌄
Answer: Respiratory failure and autonomic instability.

Case 9: Proximal Weakness with Preserved Sensation

Clinical findings

A 41-year-old woman has difficulty climbing stairs, rising from a chair and combing her hair. Hip and shoulder girdle power is reduced. Distal power is relatively preserved. Reflexes are present. Sensation is normal.

Q1Which part of the motor unit is most likely affected?⌄
Answer: Skeletal muscle, producing a proximal myopathic pattern.
Q2What examination feature would favour myopathy over peripheral neuropathy?⌄
Answer: Symmetric proximal weakness with preserved sensation and relatively preserved reflexes until advanced disease.
Q3Which additional bedside sign would suggest an inflammatory myopathy?⌄
Answer: Neck flexor weakness, characteristic skin rash, or muscle tenderness depending on the specific syndrome.

Case 10: Distal Weakness, Wasting and Stocking Sensory Loss

Clinical findings

A 62-year-old man has progressive numbness of both feet and difficulty walking. There is distal wasting, bilateral foot drop, absent ankle jerks and impaired vibration up to the knees. Upper limb reflexes are preserved.

Q1What is the pattern of neurological involvement?⌄
Answer: A length-dependent, predominantly distal sensorimotor polyneuropathy.
Q2Why are the lower limbs affected before the upper limbs?⌄
Answer: The longest axons are most vulnerable, producing a length-dependent 'dying-back' pattern.
Q3Which common causes should be sought first?⌄
Answer: Diabetes, alcohol use, vitamin B12 deficiency, renal disease, hypothyroidism, medications/toxins and paraproteinaemia depending on context.

Case 11: Spastic Paraparesis with a Sensory Level

Clinical findings

A 36-year-old man has progressive weakness of both legs for three weeks. Tone is increased, knee and ankle jerks are brisk, plantars are extensor. Pinprick sensation is reduced below the umbilicus. Upper limbs are normal.

Q1Localise the lesion.⌄
Answer: Thoracic spinal cord, approximately around the T10 dermatome level clinically.
Q2Why does a sensory level matter?⌄
Answer: It strongly supports a spinal cord lesion and helps estimate the rostrocaudal level.
Q3What urgent structural cause must be excluded?⌄
Answer: Spinal cord compression from tumour, epidural abscess, vertebral disease or other mass lesion.

Case 12: Ipsilateral Weakness and Contralateral Pain Loss

Clinical findings

After a penetrating injury to the left side of the thoracic spine, the patient has left-sided leg weakness with loss of vibration and joint position sense on the left, but loss of pain and temperature on the right below the lesion.

Q1What spinal cord pattern is this?⌄
Answer: A hemicord syndrome.
Q2Why are pain and temperature lost on the opposite side?⌄
Answer: Spinothalamic fibres cross within one to two spinal segments after entering the cord.
Q3Why are vibration and proprioception lost on the same side?⌄
Answer: Posterior column fibres ascend ipsilaterally in the spinal cord and cross only in the medulla.

Case 13: Saddle Sensory Loss with Early Bladder Dysfunction

Clinical findings

A 55-year-old develops acute urinary retention, bilateral leg weakness and numbness around the perineum. Knee jerks are preserved, ankle jerks are reduced. Anal tone is diminished.

Q1Which anatomical region is most concerning?⌄
Answer: The conus medullaris or cauda equina region.
Q2Which features favour conus involvement over a more distal cauda equina lesion?⌄
Answer: Early bladder/bowel dysfunction, relatively symmetric deficits, prominent saddle anaesthesia and mixed upper/lower motor neuron features.
Q3What is the immediate next step?⌄
Answer: Urgent MRI of the lumbosacral spine and neurosurgical/spinal evaluation if compression is suspected.

Case 14: Gait Ataxia with Loss of Vibration Sense

Clinical findings

A 67-year-old man walks with a broad-based, stamping gait. He is much worse when his eyes are closed. Vibration and joint position sense are reduced in both feet. Finger-nose testing is normal.

Q1Is this cerebellar or sensory ataxia?⌄
Answer: Sensory ataxia.
Q2What bedside sign supports this?⌄
Answer: A positive Romberg sign with impaired posterior column or large-fibre sensory function.
Q3Why is finger-nose testing relatively preserved?⌄
Answer: The cerebellum is functioning; the principal deficit is loss of proprioceptive input from the limbs.

Case 15: Limb Incoordination with Normal Power and Sensation

Clinical findings

A 43-year-old woman has unsteady gait and slurred speech. Power and primary sensation are normal. Finger-nose testing shows past-pointing, heel-knee-shin testing is impaired, and there is dysdiadochokinesia on the left.

Q1Where is the lesion localised?⌄
Answer: Left cerebellar hemisphere.
Q2Why are limb signs usually ipsilateral in cerebellar disease?⌄
Answer: Cerebellar pathways effectively influence the ipsilateral side because of double-crossing in major motor circuits.
Q3Which additional eye movement abnormality might be seen?⌄
Answer: Gaze-evoked nystagmus or other cerebellar ocular motor abnormalities.

Case 16: Rest Tremor, Rigidity and Reduced Arm Swing

Clinical findings

A 69-year-old man has slowness of movement, reduced facial expression, a 4–6 Hz rest tremor of one hand, cogwheel rigidity and reduced arm swing on the same side. Power is normal.

Q1Which motor system is primarily involved?⌄
Answer: The extrapyramidal/basal ganglia system.
Q2Which finding is essential for the clinical syndrome?⌄
Answer: Bradykinesia, accompanied by rest tremor and/or rigidity.
Q3Which feature would make you question a typical idiopathic presentation?⌄
Answer: Early falls, prominent autonomic failure, vertical gaze palsy, early severe dementia, pyramidal signs or poor levodopa response.

Case 17: Progressive Weakness with Wasting and Brisk Reflexes

Clinical findings

A 58-year-old man has progressive weakness of both hands and difficulty walking. There is intrinsic hand muscle wasting and fasciculation, but knee reflexes are brisk and plantars are extensor. Sensation is normal.

Q1Which two motor systems appear to be involved simultaneously?⌄
Answer: Lower motor neurons and upper motor neurons.
Q2What broad disease category does this pattern suggest?⌄
Answer: Motor neuron disease, once mimics are excluded.
Q3Which finding would argue against a pure motor neuron disorder?⌄
Answer: Objective sensory loss, sphincter dysfunction, prominent ocular movement abnormalities, or a clear sensory level.

Case 18: Acute Vertigo with Severe Gait Unsteadiness

Clinical findings

A 61-year-old hypertensive man develops continuous vertigo, vomiting and inability to walk without support. There is direction-changing gaze-evoked nystagmus and mild ipsilateral limb ataxia. Hearing is normal.

Q1Does this pattern favour a peripheral or central vestibular lesion?⌄
Answer: A central lesion.
Q2Which bedside findings are particularly concerning for a central cause?⌄
Answer: Severe truncal/gait ataxia, direction-changing nystagmus, skew deviation, focal neurological signs or an abnormal head impulse pattern in the appropriate setting.
Q3What vascular territory should be considered?⌄
Answer: Posterior circulation, especially cerebellar or brainstem ischemia.

Case 19: Headache, Vomiting and Bilateral Disc Swelling

Clinical findings

A 34-year-old woman has progressively worsening morning headache and vomiting for several weeks. Fundus examination shows bilateral optic disc swelling. She now has horizontal diplopia due to impaired abduction of one eye.

Q1What physiological syndrome is suggested?⌄
Answer: Raised intracranial pressure.
Q2Why can abduction weakness occur even when the primary lesion is elsewhere?⌄
Answer: A sixth nerve palsy can occur as a false-localising sign due to stretching of the abducens nerve.
Q3What must be done before lumbar puncture if a mass lesion is possible?⌄
Answer: Urgent neuroimaging should be performed first to assess for a space-occupying lesion, obstructive hydrocephalus or dangerous mass effect.

Case 20: Recurrent Brief Jerking of One Hand with Preserved Awareness

Clinical findings

A 27-year-old has recurrent episodes beginning with rhythmic jerking of the right thumb, spreading over 30 seconds to the hand and forearm. Awareness is preserved. After some episodes, the right hand remains weak for 10–20 minutes.

Q1What type of neurological event is this?⌄
Answer: A focal motor seizure with preserved awareness and Jacksonian spread.
Q2Where would you localise the cortical focus?⌄
Answer: The contralateral left primary motor cortex, in the hand area of the precentral gyrus.
Q3What explains the transient post-event weakness?⌄
Answer: Todd paralysis, a temporary postictal focal neurological deficit.
