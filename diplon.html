Case Summary (Positive Findings):

  • Demographics & Risk Factors: 76-year-old male with a 10-year history of hypertension (on Telma-AM).
  • Acute Onset Neurological Symptoms (1 day duration):
    • Vertigo: Sudden onset, continuous, spontaneous, non-progressive, described as "surroundings spinning." Associated with difficulty in standing. No associated light-headedness, nausea/vomiting, hearing loss, tinnitus, headache, or neck pain.
    • Diplopia: Sudden onset, binocular, horizontal, constant, disappears on closing either eye. Associated ptosis appears to be documented. No associated pupillary abnormality, eye pain, or facial numbness.
    • Dysarthria: Sudden onset, constant slurring of speech (difficulty in articulation of words). No associated hoarseness, nasal voice, dysphagia, nasal regurgitation, coughing while eating, facial deviation, or tongue deviation.
  • Past Medical History: Hypertension (10 years), hernioplasty (2023), ORIF left ankle (2019), bilateral cataract (1 month, context unclear).

Probability-Based Differential Diagnoses (on the basis of History):

  1. Ischemic Stroke (Brainstem/Cerebellar) (60%): Acute onset of multiple focal neurological deficits (vertigo, dysarthria, binocular horizontal diplopia with ptosis, difficulty standing) in an elderly patient with a significant vascular risk factor (hypertension). This constellation strongly localizes to the posterior circulation (brainstem and/or cerebellum), where ischemia can cause central vertigo, ataxia, dysarthria, and cranial nerve palsies (e.g., partial CN III palsy from midbrain ischemia). The duration of 1 day makes stroke highly probable.
  2. Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA) (15%): Similar to ischemic stroke in presentation, involving the posterior circulation. The symptoms are acute and focal. If these symptoms were to resolve completely within 24 hours (or typically much sooner), it would be classified as a TIA. Given the 1-day duration, it's still a possibility, but the persistence makes stroke more likely.
  3. Myasthenia Gravis (MG) Crisis/Exacerbation (10%): MG can cause fluctuating ptosis and binocular diplopia (often horizontal), and bulbar symptoms like dysarthria. The absence of pupillary involvement is consistent. While the "sudden onset" and "constant" nature might argue against typical MG fluctuations, an acute exacerbation or crisis can present this way. However, the prominent vertigo and difficulty standing (suggesting ataxia rather than pure weakness) are less typical for MG.
  4. Vertebrobasilar Insufficiency (VBI) (10%): This refers to transient or chronic reduction in blood flow to the posterior circulation. It can manifest with symptoms like vertigo, diplopia, dysarthria, and ataxia, fitting the patient's presentation. While often leading to TIA or stroke, VBI can be considered as the underlying pathophysiological process causing these symptoms.
  5. Brain Tumor (Posterior Fossa/Brainstem) (5%): A mass lesion in the posterior fossa (cerebellum or brainstem) could cause these symptoms due to compression or direct involvement. However, the "sudden onset" and "1 day" duration make an acute vascular event much more likely than a de novo presentation of a tumor, which typically has a more subacute or progressive course. An acute hemorrhage into a tumor is a less common but possible acute presentation.

]Q1 How do you clinically differentiate true vertigo from lightheadedness or presyncope in the patient's history?
MODEL_ANS1 Vertigo is defined as a subjective hallucination of movement—an illusion of rotation or spinning of the surroundings or the body itself, resulting from an imbalance in the vestibular pathways. In contrast, lightheadedness or presyncope is an ill-defined feeling of faintness, giddiness, unsteadiness, or impending loss of consciousness without any rotational illusion, typically caused by global cerebral hypoperfusion, orthostatic hypotension, or cardiac arrhythmias. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Part I, Long Cases: Hemiplegia, p. 131; Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Nervous System: Cranial Nerves, p. 614).

Q2 What features in the clinical history distinguish central vertigo from peripheral (labyrinthine/vestibular nerve) vertigo?
MODEL_ANS2 Peripheral vertigo is typically episodic, severe, accompanied by prominent auditory symptoms (tinnitus, hearing loss, fullness in the ear), and marked autonomic features (nausea, vomiting, pallor, diaphoresis), but lacks associated central neurological deficits. Central vertigo (due to brainstem or cerebellar involvement) is often continuous, less severe in rotational intensity, usually lacks tinnitus or hearing loss, and is characteristically accompanied by brainstem or cerebellar signs such as diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, ataxia, or crossed sensorimotor deficits. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Nervous System: Vertigo, pp. 600, 614–615; Kundu, Part I, Spot Cases: Cranial Nerves, p. 450).

Q3 Why is it critical to establish whether diplopia is binocular or monocular in the history, and how do you differentiate them?
MODEL_ANS3 Binocular diplopia arises from ocular misalignment due to extraocular muscle paresis (cranial nerves III, IV, or VI palsy) or brainstem disruption of conjugate gaze; it characteristically disappears completely when either eye is occluded. Monocular diplopia persists even when the unaffected eye is covered and indicates an intrinsic ocular/refractive abnormality (such as cataract, severe astigmatism, or subluxated lens) or, rarely, a psychogenic or primary visual cortex lesion. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Nervous System: Ocular Movements, p. 599; Kundu, Part I, Spot Cases: Cranial Nerves, p. 445).

Q4 The patient presents with sudden slurring of speech. How do you distinguish dysarthria from dysphasia/aphasia by history?
MODEL_ANS4 Dysarthria is a motor disorder of articulation and pronunciation of spoken phonemes/syllables caused by neuromuscular weakness or incoordination of the speech apparatus (lips, tongue, palate, larynx), with intact language comprehension, reading, and writing. Dysphasia/aphasia is a higher cortical processing deficit involving language formulation, comprehension, naming, or repetition resulting from damage to dominant cortical speech areas (Broca's, Wernicke's). (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Nervous System: Speech and Language, pp. 585–586; Kundu, Part I, Spot Cases: Speech Disturbances, pp. 438–439).

Q5 What anatomical vascular territory is implicated when a patient presents with the triad of acute vertigo, binocular diplopia, and dysarthria?
MODEL_ANS5 This clinical triad indicates vertebrobasilar arterial territory (posterior circulation) ischemia. The vertebrobasilar system supplies the brainstem (midbrain, pons, medulla), cerebellum, and occipital lobes. Ischemia involving the vestibular nuclei/cerebellar connections produces vertigo; involvement of the ocular motor nuclei (CN III, IV, VI) or medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF) causes diplopia; and ischemia affecting the corticobulbar fibers or lower cranial nerve nuclei produces dysarthria. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Nervous System: Stroke and Cerebrovascular Disorders, p. 667; Kundu, Part I, Long Cases: Hemiplegia, p. 131).

Q6 Why must the exact "last known well" time and time of onset be established in an acute stroke presentation?
MODEL_ANS6 Determining the exact onset time or "last known well" time defines the therapeutic window for acute reperfusion strategies. Intravenous thrombolysis (IV rtPA) is indicated within 4.5 hours of symptom onset, and mechanical thrombectomy can be considered up to 6–24 hours in selected anterior or posterior circulation large vessel occlusions. Knowing the exact onset also differentiates acute vascular events (maximal at onset) from space-occupying lesions or inflammatory demyelination. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Nervous System: Stroke Management, pp. 674–676; Kundu, Part I, Long Cases: Hemiplegia, pp. 131, 139).

Q7 What is the clinical significance of inquiring about "drop attacks" or transient syncopal spells in suspected vertebrobasilar insufficiency?
MODEL_ANS7 A drop attack is a sudden, unexpected fall to the ground due to sudden loss of lower limb postural muscle tone without alteration or loss of consciousness. It is characteristic of transient ischemia to the brainstem reticular formation and corticospinal tracts within the vertebrobasilar arterial distribution. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Part I, Long Cases: Hemiplegia, p. 131; Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Nervous System: Vertebrobasilar Insufficiency, p. 667).

Q8 The patient is noted to have ptosis in association with diplopia. How do you distinguish third cranial nerve palsy from Horner's syndrome on history and examination?
MODEL_ANS8 In a third nerve palsy, ptosis is typically moderate to complete (due to levator palpebrae superioris paralysis), extraocular movements are restricted (eye lies down and out), and the pupil is classically dilated and fixed (mydriasis) if parasympathetic fibers are involved. In Horner's syndrome (oculosympathetic paresis), the ptosis is mild/partial (due to paralysis of Müller's superior tarsal muscle), ocular motility is normal, the pupil is constricted (miosis), and there may be associated ipsilateral facial anhidrosis and enophthalmos. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Nervous System: Cranial Nerves, pp. 599–600; Kundu, Part I, Spot Cases: Cranial Nerves, pp. 439, 445).

Q9 Why must one systematically inquire about dysphagia, dysphonia, and nasal regurgitation in suspected posterior circulation stroke?
MODEL_ANS9 Dysphagia (difficulty swallowing), dysphonia/hoarseness, and nasal regurgitation of fluids indicate involvement of the lower cranial nerves (IX, X, and XI / nucleus ambiguus) or their corticobulbar projections. Their presence signifies medullary ischemia (such as lateral medullary syndrome / Wallenberg's syndrome from PICA or vertebral artery occlusion) and flags a life-threatening risk of aspiration pneumonia and airway compromise. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Nervous System: Stroke Syndromes, p. 667; Kundu, Part I, Spot Cases: Bulbar Palsy, p. 438).

Q10 What is the clinical importance of asking about preceding neck pain, occipital headache, or recent minor cervical trauma?
MODEL_ANS10 Severe acute occipital or neck pain preceding posterior circulation symptoms in an elderly or middle-aged patient strongly raises suspicion for vertebral artery dissection or cerebellar infarction/hemorrhage. Vertebral artery dissection can lead to intramural hematoma, thromboembolism, or basilar artery occlusion. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Nervous System: Cerebrovascular Disorders, p. 667; Kundu, Part I, Long Cases: Hemiplegia, p. 131).

Q11 Why is it necessary to ask about sensory loss over the ipsilateral face and contralateral half of the body ("crossed hemisensory loss")?
MODEL_ANS11 Crossed sensory loss is pathognomonic for a brainstem lesion (classically lateral medullary or lower pontine infarction). It occurs because descending spinal tract and nucleus of the trigeminal nerve (supplying pain and temperature to the ipsilateral face) and the ascending lateral spinothalamic tract (carrying pain and temperature from the contralateral trunk and limbs after decussation in the spinal cord) run in close anatomical proximity in the dorsolateral brainstem. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Nervous System: Brainstem Syndromes, p. 667; Kundu, Part I, Long Cases: Hemiplegia, p. 137).

Q12 Why is a thorough cardiac history (palpitations, chest pain, exertional dyspnea) essential in this elderly patient?
MODEL_ANS12 Vertebrobasilar strokes can be cardioembolic in up to 20–30% of cases. History of palpitations suggests paroxysmal or persistent atrial fibrillation; chest pain points toward acute myocardial infarction with mural thrombus; and exertional dyspnea may indicate rheumatic valvular heart disease, dilated cardiomyopathy, or left ventricular dysfunction, all of which are major embolic substrates. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Cardiovascular System, p. 288; Kundu, Part I, Long Cases: Hemiplegia, pp. 133–134).

Q13 How does long-standing hypertension (10 years) pathophysiologically contribute to ischemic stroke in the vertebrobasilar territory?
MODEL_ANS13 Chronic hypertension promotes large-vessel accelerated atherosclerosis in the subclavian, vertebral, and basilar arteries (causing atherothrombosis or artery-to-artery embolism) and induces lipohyalinosis/microatheroma in small deep penetrating branch arteries (paramedian and circumferential branches of the basilar artery), resulting in lacunar infarctions of the pons, medulla, and midbrain. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Nervous System: Cerebrovascular Disease, p. 674; Kundu, Part I, Long Cases: Hemiplegia, p. 140).

Q14 What is a Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA), and why is history of preceding stereotyped neurological episodes relevant?
MODEL_ANS14 A TIA is a transient episode of neurological dysfunction caused by focal brain, spinal cord, or retinal ischemia without acute tissue infarction, traditionally resolving within 24 hours (most within 1 hour). Preceding brief episodes of vertigo, diplopia, or ataxia indicate vertebrobasilar insufficiency and carry a very high short-term risk of a completed disabling posterior circulation stroke. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Nervous System: TIA, p. 667; Kundu, Part I, Long Cases: Hemiplegia, p. 142).

Q15 What does the inability to stand or balance in the absence of limb weakness indicate regarding localization?
MODEL_ANS15 Marked inability to stand or balance without significant limb weakness signifies cerebellar ataxia (involving the vermis/vestibulocerebellum) or severe vestibulospinal tract dysfunction. It differentiates an axial or midline cerebellar/vestibular deficit from a pure corticospinal (pyramidal) motor hemiparesis. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Nervous System: Cerebellar Function, p. 615; Kundu, Part II, Spot Cases: Cerebellar Disorders, p. 278).
