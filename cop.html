Stage 1: History
A 21-year-old female college student from New Delhi presents to the emergency department with a 10-day history of progressive fatigue, weakness, light-headedness, exertional dyspnea, and dark tea-colored urine, culminating in a brief syncopal episode without injury. She reports no use of tobacco, alcohol, herbal supplements, or illicit substances. She is not sexually active, has no recent travel history, and has no family history of metabolic, autoimmune, or hematologic disorders.
Review of Systems: No fever prior to admission, no chest pain, cough, skin rash, joint pains, or abdominal pain.

Resident:

Hello, Chief Resident here.

Key Findings: A 21-year-old female presenting with acute symptomatic anemia (fatigue, dyspnea, syncope) and dark tea-colored urine, strongly indicating severe hemolysis (intravascular or extravascular) without active bleeding.

Differential Diagnoses:
1. Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA) (40%): Classic presentation in a young female for warm/cold AIHA leading to rapid red cell destruction and hemoglobinuria.
2. G6PD Deficiency (25%): Acute hemolytic crisis triggered by an unrecorded oxidative stress, infection, or food item.
3. Infectious Hemolysis (e.g., Malaria / Blackwater Fever) (15%): High relevance given residency in New Delhi; P. falciparum can cause profound intravascular hemolysis.
4. Paroxysmal Nocturnal Hemoglobinuria (PNH) (10%): Acquired stem cell disorder presenting with episodic dark urine and anemia in young adults.
5. Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TTP/HUS) (10%): Urgent exclusion required for microangiopathic hemolytic anemia despite the absence of neurological or renal symptoms.

Recommended Initial Tests:
1. CBC with Reticulocyte Count & Peripheral Smear (evaluating spherocytes, schistocytes, bite cells).
2. Hemolysis Markers: LDH, Haptoglobin, Indirect Bilirubin, Urine Dipstick & Hemosiderin.
3. Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT / Direct Coombs Test).
4. Malaria Smear & Rapid Diagnostic Test (RDT).
5. G6PD Enzyme Assay.

Expert:

Hello, Expert Clinician here.

Best current unifying diagnosis: Acute symptomatic intravascular hemolytic anemia with hemoglobinuria; paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) is the leading etiologic consideration given unexplained tea-colored urine and anemia in a young adult, but confirmation is essential.

Top differentials
- Warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia: Commoner than PNH in young women; favored by spherocytes and positive DAT, although hemoglobinuria is less characteristic.
- G6PD-related hemolysis: Fits acute intravascular hemolysis, but no oxidative trigger is reported and clinically significant disease is less common in females; testing during crisis may be falsely normal.
- TTP/other thrombotic microangiopathy: Critical not to miss, but currently lacks documented thrombocytopenia, renal injury, neurologic abnormalities, or schistocytes.
- Malaria: Geographic exposure is relevant, but absent fever or recent travel lowers probability.

Key pivot: Confirm whether dark urine represents hemoglobinuria and determine immune versus nonimmune hemolysis.

Immediate move: Obtain CBC/platelets, reticulocytes, smear, bilirubin, LDH, haptoglobin, creatinine, urinalysis with microscopy, and DAT; type/crossmatch and transfuse if unstable or severely anemic. If DAT-negative intravascular hemolysis is confirmed, order high-sensitivity FLAER/CD55/CD59 flow cytometry for PNH.

Teaching pearl: Tea-colored urine with positive heme but few/no RBCs suggests hemoglobinuria or myoglobinuria—not hematuria.

Stage 5: Final Results & Clinical Outcome
Final Diagnosis:
Wilson's Disease (Fulminant Hepatic Failure with Coombs-Negative Intravascular Hemolytic Anemia and Leukemoid Reaction) secondary to compound heterozygous ATP7B gene mutations.

Pathophysiologic Mechanism:
Acute hepatocellular necrosis from copper overload releases massive free copper into the circulation. Excess ionic copper inhibits red cell glycolytic enzymes (G6PD, hexokinase) and causes direct oxidative cross-linking of erythrocyte membrane proteins, triggering acute intravascular Coombs-negative hemolysis and a leukemoid reaction. Copper-mediated oxidative damage also inactivates serum alkaline phosphatase, explaining the classic low ALP-to-bilirubin ratio.

Management & Transplantation Outcome:
- Medical Bridge & Liver Transplantation: Medical therapy with Trientine and Zinc Acetate was initiated alongside plasmapheresis and hemodialysis bridge. However, due to progressive fulminant hepatic failure, she underwent emergency Orthotopic Liver Transplantation (OLT) on Hospital Day 6 of readmission.
- Explanted Liver Pathology: Confirmed cirrhosis with heavy periseptal hepatocyte copper accumulation on rhodanine staining; dry weight liver copper content was 526 µg/g (normal 10–35 µg/g).
- Genetic Confirmation & Family Cascade Screening: Genetic testing revealed ATP7B mutations (c.331C->T stop mutation and IVS4-34G->A). Cascade screening of her 18-year-old asymptomatic sister identified low ceruloplasmin/copper and identical ATP7B mutations; early zinc acetate therapy prevented liver failure.
- Long-Term Follow-up: At 1-year post-transplant, the patient has completely normal liver and kidney function, normal cell counts, and is thriving.

Top 15 Takeaways for Clinicians

    Top 15 Takeaways for Clinicians

    1. A low ALP-to-total-bilirubin ratio (<4) with acute jaundice is a major red flag. In young adults presenting with acute liver injury, a markedly suppressed alkaline phosphatase relative to bilirubin strongly points to fulminant Wilson's disease.
    2. Wilson's disease can present as Coombs-negative intravascular hemolysis. Massive copper release from necrotic hepatocytes causes direct oxidative damage to RBC membranes and glycolytic enzymes, mimicking AIHA.
    3. Apparent response to immunosuppressants can be misleading. Transient stabilization after steroids or rituximab can occur coincidentally; a negative Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT/Coombs) should prompt investigation for non-immune hemolysis like copper toxicity.
    4. Marked leukocytosis (>40,000/µL) can represent a leukemoid reaction to severe hemolysis. Leukocytosis with left shift is common in acute copper-mediated hemolysis and does not necessarily indicate bacterial sepsis.
    5. Serum ceruloplasmin can be falsely normal or severely low. While ceruloplasmin <4 mg/dL confirms Wilson's disease, acute phase reactant responses during liver necrosis can transiently elevate ceruloplasmin; combine with 24-hr urinary copper and slit-lamp exams.
    6. Kayser-Fleischer (KF) rings are diagnostic of hepatic/CNS copper accumulation. Slit-lamp examination for golden-brown corneoscleral rings confirms Wilson's disease, though KF rings may be absent in up to 50% of fulminant hepatic presentations.
    7. 24-hour urinary copper >100 µg (or >1,000 µg in acute crisis) is diagnostic. In fulminant Wilsonian crisis, urinary copper excretion often exceeds 4,000 µg/24 hr due to massive circulating free copper.
    8. An AST-to-ALT ratio >2.2 aids in differentiating Wilsonian liver failure. Unlike viral or autoimmune hepatitis where ALT dominates, Wilson's acute liver injury features AST > ALT due to mitochondrial oxidative injury.
    9. Penicillamine should be avoided in acute neurological or hepatic deterioration. Penicillamine can paradoxically worsen neurological symptoms; use Trientine and Zinc Acetate for medical chelation, or prepare for transplantation.
    10. Therapeutic Plasma Exchange (TPE) bridges fulminant copper crisis. Plasmapheresis rapidly removes circulating free copper and reduces intravascular hemolysis while awaiting liver transplantation.
    11. Fulminant hepatic failure in Wilson's disease requires emergency liver transplantation. Medical chelation alone is ineffective in acute-on-chronic liver failure; Orthotopic Liver Transplantation (OLT) is lifesaving.
    12. Quantitative hepatic dry weight copper >250 µg/g confirms diagnosis. Explant or biopsy liver tissue showing heavy periseptal copper accumulation on rhodanine staining provides definitive histopathological proof.
    13. Cascade genetic screening of first-degree relatives saves lives. Screening siblings of index cases with ATP7B sequencing and ceruloplasmin identifies presymptomatic Wilson's disease, enabling preventative zinc therapy.
    14. Pigment nephropathy can cause acute kidney injury in copper hemolysis. Hemoglobinuria from intravascular lysis precipitates acute tubular injury, requiring hemodialysis support until liver function or transplantation stabilizes the patient.
    15. Post-transplant prognosis in Wilson's disease is excellent. Liver transplantation corrects the metabolic ATP7B defect in the liver, leading to complete long-term survival, normalization of copper kinetics, and resolution of systemic manifestations.
