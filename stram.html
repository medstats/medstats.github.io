Clinical Case Summary & Differential Diagnosis (History Phase)

Case Summary (Positive Findings):

  • Demographics/Risk Factors: 65-year-old female, known hypertensive for approximately 8 years with irregular medication use.
  • Onset/Course: "Wake-up stroke" (last known well ~9:30 PM, woke with deficits ~4 AM), sudden onset, maximal at onset, non-progressive.
  • Motor Deficits:
    • Right upper limb weakness (difficulty with routine activities).
    • Right lower limb weakness (difficulty standing/walking).
    • Deviation of angle of mouth towards the left (acute, non-progressive, no difficulty closing eyes, clinically suggestive of a central facial palsy).
  • Speech/Communication: Acute-onset slurring of speech (dysarthria), able to understand spoken language and communicate appropriately (no definite history suggestive of aphasia).
  • Associated Symptoms (Uncertain): Drooling of saliva (mentioned, but noted as marked/corrected in history).
  • Relevant Negative History (supporting specific diagnoses): No loss of consciousness, seizures, severe headache, recurrent vomiting, diplopia, dysphagia, or definite cortical language dysfunction. No preceding head trauma, TIA, chest pain, palpitations, recent fever, or anticoagulant use.

Probability-Based Differential Diagnoses (on the basis of History):

  1. Ischemic Stroke (85%): This is the most probable diagnosis given the sudden onset of focal neurological deficits (right hemiparesis, central left facial palsy, dysarthria) in a patient with a significant vascular risk factor (hypertension with irregular treatment). The "wake-up stroke" pattern, maximal deficit at onset, and the absence of severe headache, vomiting, or altered consciousness strongly favor an ischemic event.
  2. Intracerebral Hemorrhage (10%): While less likely than ischemic stroke due to the absence of classic symptoms like severe headache, vomiting, or altered consciousness, intracerebral hemorrhage can present with sudden focal neurological deficits, especially in a hypertensive patient. Smaller bleeds or those in specific locations might not cause the more dramatic symptoms.
  3. Stroke Mimic (e.g., Todd's Paralysis post-seizure, complicated migraine) (2%): Although seizures are explicitly denied, a focal seizure with post-ictal weakness (Todd's paralysis) could theoretically cause sudden, transient weakness. A complicated migraine with prolonged aura is another less common mimic, though less likely given the specific constellation of motor deficits and the patient's age.
  4. Brain Tumor (with acute event like hemorrhage or edema) (2%): While typically presenting with a more progressive course, a brain tumor can acutely manifest with focal neurological deficits due to hemorrhage within the tumor or sudden increase in peritumoral edema, thereby mimicking a stroke.
  5. Subdural Hematoma (Chronic) (1%): Although acute trauma is denied, a chronic subdural hematoma can present with subacute or even seemingly sudden neurological deficits, particularly in older individuals, sometimes without a clear history of trauma. However, the "maximal at onset" and non-progressive nature makes it less typical for a chronic SDH.

Q5 Does the absence of aphasia rule out a left hemispheric stroke?

MODEL_ANS5 No. Aphasia occurs when the dominant cortical language areas or their connections are involved; a subcortical/internal capsular lesion may produce contralateral hemiplegia and dysarthria without aphasia. MCA stem or dominant cortical involvement may produce aphasia, whereas different MCA branch or penetrating-vessel lesions have different manifestations.

Q1 What is the clinical significance of the patient's neurological deficit being noticed upon waking at 4:00 AM after being last seen normal at 9:30 PM?
MODEL_ANS1 In clinical neurology, a stroke noticed upon waking from sleep is classified as a "wake-up stroke" or thrombotic stroke. Thrombotic cerebral infarction typically develops during periods of reduced systemic blood pressure and sluggish cerebral circulation, commonly occurring during sleep or in the early morning hours. Additionally, determining the "last known well" time (9:30 PM) is critical to establish the therapeutic time window for reperfusion therapies (such as IV thrombolysis or endovascular thrombectomy). (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Part I, Case 16: Hemiplegia, p. 139; Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Nervous System, p. 674).

Q2 How do you clinically distinguish an upper motor neuron (UMN) facial nerve palsy from a lower motor neuron (LMN) facial palsy based on history?
MODEL_ANS2 An upper motor neuron (supranuclear) facial lesion spares the muscles of the upper face (frontalis, corrugator supercilii, and upper orbicularis oculi) because the upper facial subnucleus receives bilateral corticonuclear innervation. Therefore, the patient retains the ability to wrinkle the forehead and close the eyes, presenting only with lower facial weakness (flattened nasolabial fold, deviation of the angle of the mouth to the healthy side, and drooling). In contrast, an LMN lesion (nuclear or infranuclear, such as Bell's palsy) paralyzes both the upper and lower facial muscles on the ipsilateral side, resulting in the loss of forehead wrinkling and inability to close the eye (with Bell's phenomenon). (Reference: Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, p. 137; Alagappan, Nervous System, pp. 608–610).

Q3 Why did the angle of the mouth deviate towards the left side when the patient developed weakness on the right side of her body?
MODEL_ANS3 In right-sided facial weakness (UMN type), the right perioral muscles (buccinator and orbicularis oris) are paretic. The intact, unopposed contralateral (left-sided) facial muscles contract normally and pull the angle of the mouth toward the healthy (normal) left side, especially during emotional expression, talking, or smiling. (Reference: Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, p. 136; Alagappan, Nervous System, p. 608).

Q4 How do you differentiate dysarthria from dysphasia/aphasia in the clinical history?
MODEL_ANS4 Dysarthria is a disorder of articulation and pronunciation of spoken consonants caused by neuromuscular impairment of speech-producing organs (lips, tongue, palate, larynx), with intact language comprehension, reading, and written expression. Dysphasia/aphasia is a higher cortical disorder involving the understanding (comprehension) or production (fluency, syntax, repetition, naming) of language across spoken and written modalities. (Reference: Alagappan, Nervous System, Speech and Language, pp. 584–586; Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, p. 136).

Q5 Why is it necessary to enquire about dominant handedness when a patient presents with a suspected stroke and speech alteration?
MODEL_ANS5 Handedness identifies cerebral hemispheric dominance. In approximately 95% of right-handed individuals and nearly 50–70% of left-handed individuals, the left cerebral hemisphere is dominant for language functions. Therefore, right-sided hemiplegia from a left hemispheric cortical stroke is frequently accompanied by aphasia (Broca's, Wernicke's, or Global), whereas a pure dysarthria with dense hemiplegia points toward a subcortical (internal capsule) lesion. (Reference: Alagappan, Nervous System, Higher Mental Functions, p. 574; Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, p. 136).

Q6 What is the clinical significance of inquiring about convulsions or loss of consciousness at the onset of hemiplegia?
MODEL_ANS6 Severe loss of consciousness or deep coma at onset strongly suggests intracerebral hemorrhage, massive hemispheric infarction with brain herniation, or subarachnoid hemorrhage. Convulsions at onset may point to an underlying cortical lesion, hemorrhagic transformation, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, or post-ictal Todd's paresis. In typical cerebral thrombosis or lacunar infarcts, consciousness is usually preserved. (Reference: Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, pp. 136, 139; Alagappan, Nervous System, p. 676).

Q7 What are the classic features that distinguish cerebral thrombosis from cerebral embolism on history?
MODEL_ANS7 Cerebral thrombosis typically occurs in older hypertensive/atherosclerotic individuals, develops during sleep or upon waking, often progresses in a stepwise or evolving fashion over hours, and may be preceded by TIAs. Cerebral embolism has a dramatic, instantaneous ("bolt from the blue") onset during active wakefulness or exertion, reaches maximal deficit in seconds, is frequently seen in younger individuals or those with underlying cardiac disease (atrial fibrillation, rheumatic valvular disease, infective endocarditis, recent MI), and may show rapid initial clearing. (Reference: Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, p. 139; Alagappan, Nervous System, p. 668).

Q8 Why is the history of hypertension and its compliance crucial in this patient?
MODEL_ANS8 Long-standing, poorly controlled hypertension is the single most important modifiable risk factor for cerebrovascular accidents. It promotes large-vessel accelerated atherosclerosis, gives rise to lipohyalinosis and microatheromas in small penetrating arteries (causing lacunar infarcts), and predisposes to Charcot-Bouchard microaneurysms that can rupture to cause primary intracerebral hemorrhage. (Reference: Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, p. 140; Alagappan, Nervous System, p. 674).

Q9 What historical points help differentiate an internal capsule stroke from a cortical stroke?
MODEL_ANS9 An internal capsule lesion typically produces dense, proportional, complete hemiplegia (face, arm, and leg equally involved) because all corticospinal and corticonuclear fibers are tightly packed in the posterior limb and genu. In contrast, a cortical lesion (e.g., MCA or ACA territory) produces disproportionate weakness (brachial dominance in MCA, crural dominance in ACA), and is frequently associated with cortical sensory signs, aphasia, apraxia, visual field defects (homonymous hemianopia), or focal seizures. (Reference: Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, pp. 138–139; Alagappan, Nervous System, pp. 664, 674).

Q10 What is a Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA), and why must preceding episodes of TIA be documented?
MODEL_ANS10 A TIA is defined clinically as an episode of temporary, focal neurological dysfunction caused by cerebral ischemia without acute tissue infarction, historically lasting less than 24 hours (most lasting <1 hour). Preceding TIAs strongly suggest an atherothrombotic etiology and carry a high short-term risk of completed ischemic stroke, evaluated using clinical tools like the ABCD² score. (Reference: Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, p. 142; Alagappan, Nervous System, p. 663).

Q11 Why did you inquire about palpitations, chest pain, and dyspnea in a patient presenting with hemiplegia?
MODEL_ANS11 To rule out a cardiogenic source of cerebral embolism. Palpitations suggest atrial fibrillation (the commonest cardioembolic cause); chest pain suggests acute myocardial infarction with mural thrombus; and dyspnea may indicate rheumatic mitral stenosis or dilated cardiomyopathy with intracardiac thrombi. (Reference: Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, pp. 133–134; Alagappan, Cardiovascular System, p. 288).

Q12 How does asking about cranial nerve symptoms (diplopia, dysphagia, vertigo, hearing impairment) help localize the lesion?
MODEL_ANS12 Involvement of cranial nerves III through XII (causing diplopia, true vertigo, dysphagia, dysphonia, sensory loss over the face, or crossed hemiplegia) indicates a brainstem lesion (midbrain, pons, or medulla). Sparing of these functions (with only isolated UMN VII weakness) localizes the lesion supratentorially, most commonly to the contralateral internal capsule or cerebral cortex. (Reference: Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, pp. 137–139; Alagappan, Nervous System, p. 668).

Q13 What is the relevance of inquiring about trauma, anticoagulant therapy, or NSAID use in acute hemiplegia?
MODEL_ANS13 Head trauma points toward traumatic intracranial hematomas (epidural, subdural, or intracerebral contusions). Anticoagulant and antiplatelet drug use increases the index of suspicion for primary or secondary intracerebral hemorrhage and determines whether thrombolysis is safe or contraindicated. (Reference: Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, p. 140; Alagappan, Nervous System, p. 675).

Q14 Why is it necessary to enquire about bowel and bladder control in the history of a hemiplegic patient?
MODEL_ANS14 During the acute stage of "cerebral shock" (neural shock), transient urinary retention or spontaneous incontinence may occur due to temporary supraspinal reflex depression. Persistent neurogenic bladder dysfunction from stroke onset is common with bilateral lesions, parasagittal/anterior cerebral artery territory infarction, or normal pressure hydrocephalus. Unilateral hemispheric or capsular lesions usually do not lead to permanent urinary retention. (Reference: Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, pp. 136–137; Alagappan, Nervous System, p. 664).

Q15 What features in the history help distinguish an acute ischemic stroke from a Space-Occupying Lesion (SOL) or chronic subdural hematoma presenting as hemiplegia?
MODEL_ANS15 An acute ischemic stroke characteristically has a sudden, maximal onset evolving over seconds to minutes/hours. Intracranial SOLs (tumors, abscesses) and chronic subdural hematomas present with an insidious, subacute, or gradually progressive course over weeks to months, typically accompanied by early symptoms of raised intracranial tension (progressive early-morning dull headache, projectile vomiting, papilledema, and fluctuating sensorium). (Reference: Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, pp. 133, 143; Alagappan, Nervous System, p. 682).

Q1 In your cranial nerve examination, you noted reduced movement in the right frontalis and orbicularis oculi along with lower facial weakness, yet in history you favored an upper motor neuron (supranuclear) facial palsy. How do you resolve this discrepancy?
MODEL_ANS1 In a true UMN (supranuclear) facial palsy, frontalis and upper orbicularis oculi are spared because the upper facial nucleus in the pons receives bilateral corticonuclear innervation. Documenting weakness of all facial divisions (frontalis, orbicularis oculi, buccinator, and orbicularis oris) implies a lower motor neuron (LMN) facial palsy. If this were truly an LMN VII palsy associated with contralateral (right) hemiplegia, it would represent Millard-Gubler syndrome (a pontine tegmental lesion). However, if the lesion is supratentorial/capsular (producing right hemiplegia and right UMN facial palsy), the forehead wrinkling and eye closure must be preserved on the right. (Reference: Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, pp. 137–138; Alagappan, Nervous System, pp. 608–610).

Q2 You documented left lateral rectus weakness (CN VI palsy) in a patient with right-sided hemiparesis. What anatomical syndrome does this combination classically suggest?
MODEL_ANS2 An ipsilateral 6th cranial nerve palsy (left lateral rectus weakness) combined with contralateral (right-sided) hemiplegia constitutes Millard-Gubler syndrome (or Raymond syndrome/Foville syndrome when associated with 7th nerve palsy or conjugate gaze palsy), localizing the lesion to the ventrolateral (lower) pons. However, abducens palsy can also occur as a "false localizing sign" secondary to raised intracranial pressure. (Reference: Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, p. 138; Alagappan, Nervous System, pp. 604, 668).

Q3 How do you interpret the finding of anisocoria (right pupil 1 mm, left pupil 3 mm) in this clinical setting?
MODEL_ANS3 A unilateral miosis (right pupil 1 mm) suggests ipsilateral sympathetic chain disruption (partial Horner's syndrome), which can be seen in lateral medullary syndrome (Wallenberg syndrome) or internal carotid artery dissection. Conversely, a relative left mydriasis (3 mm vs 1 mm) requires ruling out an evolving 3rd nerve palsy (uncal herniation) or previous ophthalmic surgery/pharmacological dilation. Because this patient had bilateral cataract surgery (pseudophakia), surgical iris trauma or pupillary distortion must also be excluded. (Reference: Alagappan, Nervous System, Examination of Pupils, pp. 598–601; Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, p. 137).

Q4 The motor examination shows MRC Grade 2–3/5 power on the right side, but muscle tone, deep tendon reflexes, and superficial reflexes (plantar, abdominal) are not documented. What reflex findings do you expect in this patient at 24 hours post-stroke?
MODEL_ANS4 At 24 hours after an acute stroke, the patient is often still in the stage of cerebral (flaccid) shock. The affected limbs typically exhibit hypotonia and diminished/depressed deep tendon reflexes (biceps, triceps, supinator, knee, and ankle jerks). However, the Babinski sign (extensor plantar response) and loss of superficial abdominal and cremasteric reflexes on the right side are present from the onset. As shock resolves over days to weeks, classic UMN spasticity (clasp-knife rigidity), hyperreflexia, and clonus supervene. (Reference: Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, pp. 136–137; Alagappan, Nervous System, pp. 628, 638).

Q5 What is the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale for grading muscle power, and what does Grade 2/5 power specifically mean?
MODEL_ANS5 The MRC grading scale is:

Grade 0: Complete paralysis (no visible or palpable contraction).

Grade 1: Flicker or trace of contraction without joint movement.

Grade 2: Active movement with gravity eliminated.

Grade 3: Active movement against gravity but not against resistance.

Grade 4: Active movement against gravity and moderate resistance (4-, 4, 4+).

Grade 5: Normal power against full resistance.
Grade 2/5 indicates the patient can only move the joint horizontally when gravity is neutralized. (Reference: Alagappan, Nervous System, Motor System, p. 626; Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, p. 135).

Q6 Why is distal muscle weakness (hand grip poor, wrist 2/5 vs shoulder 3/5) more pronounced than proximal weakness in a corticospinal tract lesion?
MODEL_ANS6 Corticospinal (pyramidal) projections have the highest density of monosynaptic connections onto motor neurons innervating distal extremities (fingers, wrist, intrinsic hand muscles) responsible for fine, fractionated voluntary movements. Proximal muscles receive denser bilateral corticobulbar/corticospinal and extrapyramidal vestibulospinal/reticulospinal innervation, making distal movements significantly more vulnerable to unilateral pyramidal damage. (Reference: Alagappan, Nervous System, Motor System, p. 626; Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, p. 135).

Q7 What is the predilection pattern of spasticity and weakness in the upper and lower limbs in classical UMN hemiplegia (Wernicke-Mann pattern)?
MODEL_ANS7 In chronic UMN hemiplegia, weakness predominantly affects the extensors of the upper limb (shoulder abductors, elbow/wrist/finger extensors) and the flexors of the lower limb (hip/knee flexors and ankle dorsiflexors). Conversely, hypertonia (spasticity) predominates in the flexors of the upper limb and the extensors of the lower limb (anti-gravity muscles), giving rise to the characteristic hemiplegic posture and circumductory (Wernicke-Mann) gait. (Reference: Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, pp. 134, 137; Alagappan, Nervous System, p. 628).

Q8 How do you elicit the Babinski sign, what is the normal response, and what constitutes a true positive pathological response?
MODEL_ANS8 The Babinski sign is elicited by firmly stroking the lateral border of the sole of the foot from the heel toward the base of the 5th metatarsal and curving medially toward the base of the great toe using a blunt object. The normal response is plantar flexion of all toes. A positive (pathological) response is slow, tonic extension (dorsiflexion) of the great toe, often accompanied by fanning (abduction) of the outer four toes and flexion at the knee and hip (triple flexion response), indicating a corticospinal tract lesion. (Reference: Alagappan, Nervous System, Superficial Reflexes, pp. 638–640; Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, p. 137).

Q9 Name four surrogate/alternative signs for eliciting the extensor plantar response when the patient is hypersensitive or withdraws the limb.
MODEL_ANS9

Chaddock's sign: Stroking the skin beneath and around the lateral malleolus.

Oppenheim's sign: Firmly dragging the thumb and index finger down the anterior border of the tibia.

Gordon's sign: Firmly squeezing the calf muscles.

Schaeffer's sign: Firmly squeezing the Achilles tendon.
(Reference: Alagappan, Nervous System, Superficial Reflexes, p. 640; Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, p. 137).

Q10 You documented right-sided reduction in pain, temperature, and vibration sensations. What is the anatomical pathway for pain/temperature vs vibration/joint position sense?
MODEL_ANS10 Pain and temperature sensations are carried by small unmyelinated/poorly myelinated fibers (A-delta and C), enter the dorsal horn, decussate immediately (within 1–2 spinal segments) via the anterior white commissure, and ascend in the lateral spinothalamic tract to the ventral posterolateral (VPL) nucleus of the thalamus. Vibration, fine touch, and conscious proprioception ascend ipsilaterally without crossing in the posterior columns (fasciculus gracilis and cuneatus) to the medulla (nuclei gracilis and cuneatus), where second-order axons decussate as internal arcuate fibers (forming the medial lemniscus) and ascend to the VPL thalamus. Hemisensory loss for all modalities indicates a lesion at or above the thalamus/posterior limb of the internal capsule. (Reference: Alagappan, Nervous System, Sensory System, pp. 645–648; Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, p. 139).

Q11 Why was the cerebellar examination crossed out/omitted, and how should a clinician approach cerebellar testing in a hemiparetic limb?
MODEL_ANS11 Cerebellar testing (finger-nose, dysdiadochokinesia, heel-knee-shin) requires a minimum motor power of Grade 3+/5 to be interpretable; marked motor weakness (Grade 2/5) produces clumsiness and pseudo-ataxia due to weakness alone, rendering conventional tests untestable or false-positive. In such patients, cerebellar testing must be rigorously performed on the intact/normal limb (left side) and axial cerebellar function (nystagmus, titubation, dysarthria) evaluated. (Reference: Kundu, Part II, Cerebellar Disorders, p. 278; Alagappan, Nervous System, Cerebellar Function, pp. 653–656).

Q12 How do you test the 5th cranial nerve motor function at the bedside?
MODEL_ANS12

Temporalis and Masseter inspection & palpation: Ask the patient to clench their teeth firmly while palpating the muscle belly bulk and firmness.

Pterygoids: Ask the patient to open their mouth wide against resistance (in unilateral pterygoid weakness, the jaw deviates toward the paralyzed side due to unopposed action of the contralateral lateral pterygoid) and move the jaw from side to side against resistance.

Jaw jerk (mandibular reflex): Tap the examiner’s finger placed horizontally across the relaxed chin with a reflex hammer. (Reference: Alagappan, Nervous System, Cranial Nerves, pp. 605–607; Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, p. 137).

Q13 How do you clinically examine for subtle hemineglect or visual/sensory extinction (inattention) in a conscious stroke patient?
MODEL_ANS13

Sensory Extinction (Double Simultaneous Stimulation): Touch the patient simultaneously on homologous sites on both sides (e.g., both forearms or both cheeks) with eyes closed. If the patient perceives bilateral stimuli individually but ignores the right-sided stimulus during simultaneous stimulation, sensory extinction is present.

Visual Neglect/Extinction: Simultaneously present moving fingers in both visual hemifields; extinction occurs if the patient fails to notice the stimulus on the affected side.

Visuospatial Neglect: Assessed via line bisection, clock drawing, or cancellation tests. (Reference: Alagappan, Nervous System, Higher Mental Functions, pp. 578, 649; Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, p. 136).

Q14 What vital signs and cardiovascular examination findings are paramount in this hypertensive stroke patient?
MODEL_ANS14

Carotid Arteries: Auscultation for carotid bruits at the carotid bifurcations. (Reference: Kundu, Part I, Hemiplegia, pp. 133–134; Alagappan, Cardiovascular System, pp. 240–250).

Q15 What features on fundoscopic examination are essential to look for in this 65-year-old hypertensive stroke patient?
MODEL_ANS15

Hypertensive Retinopathy: Arteriolar narrowing, copper/silver wiring, AV nipping (Salus/Gunn signs), flame-shaped hemorrhages, soft (cotton-wool) exudates, and hard exudates (Keith-Wagener-Barker classification).
