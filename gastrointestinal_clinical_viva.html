Case 1: Progressive Abdominal Distension with Jaundice

Clinical findings

A 44-year-old man has progressive abdominal distension, easy fatigability and recent jaundice. Examination shows spider naevi, palmar erythema, bilateral pedal oedema and shifting dullness. The liver is small and firm; the spleen is palpable.

Q1What broad syndrome is present?⌄
Answer: Chronic liver disease with portal hypertension and decompensation, evidenced by ascites, splenomegaly and stigmata of chronic liver disease.
Q2Which findings suggest portal hypertension?⌄
Answer: Splenomegaly, ascites, dilated abdominal wall veins, thrombocytopenia if known, and variceal bleeding history.
Q3What features would indicate further decompensation?⌄
Answer: Hepatic encephalopathy, variceal bleeding, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, hepatorenal syndrome or worsening synthetic dysfunction.

Case 2: Ascites with Prominent Neck Veins and Hepatomegaly

Clinical findings

A 52-year-old man has abdominal distension and pedal oedema. JVP is markedly elevated with prominent y descents. The liver is enlarged and mildly tender. Ascites is present, but there are few stigmata of chronic liver disease.

Q1Is the ascites more likely hepatic or cardiac in origin?⌄
Answer: Cardiac or congestive ascites is more likely.
Q2Which bedside clue is most useful?⌄
Answer: Markedly elevated JVP strongly suggests systemic venous congestion rather than isolated portal hypertension.
Q3What liver-related biochemical pattern may occur in chronic congestion?⌄
Answer: Mild transaminase elevation, cholestatic enzyme elevation and hyperbilirubinaemia; severe acute congestion can cause marked aminotransferase elevation.

Case 3: Tender Hepatomegaly with New-Onset Ascites

Clinical findings

A 34-year-old woman develops abdominal pain, rapidly progressive ascites and tender hepatomegaly over two weeks. JVP is not elevated. There is no long-standing history of liver disease.

Q1Which vascular hepatic disorder should be suspected?⌄
Answer: Hepatic venous outflow obstruction, classically Budd-Chiari syndrome.
Q2What is the classic clinical triad?⌄
Answer: Abdominal pain, ascites and hepatomegaly.
Q3Which predisposing conditions should be sought?⌄
Answer: Myeloproliferative disorders, thrombophilias, pregnancy/postpartum state, oral contraceptive use and malignancy.

Case 4: Confusion, Flapping Tremor and Chronic Liver Signs

Clinical findings

A 58-year-old man with known chronic liver disease becomes drowsy and disoriented. There is fetor hepaticus and a coarse flapping tremor when the wrists are extended. He has ascites and mild jaundice.

Q1What complication is suggested?⌄
Answer: Hepatic encephalopathy.
Q2What is the flapping movement called?⌄
Answer: Asterixis.
Q3Name common precipitants that should be actively searched for.⌄
Answer: GI bleeding, infection, constipation, excess diuretics, dehydration, electrolyte disturbance, sedatives and renal dysfunction.

Case 5: Massive Upper GI Bleeding with Splenomegaly

Clinical findings

A 49-year-old man presents with haematemesis and melena. He is tachycardic. The spleen is palpable, there are dilated abdominal wall veins and mild ascites. There is no history of NSAID use.

Q1What source of bleeding is most likely?⌄
Answer: Variceal upper gastrointestinal bleeding due to portal hypertension.
Q2What are the immediate priorities before definitive endoscopy?⌄
Answer: Airway protection if needed, haemodynamic resuscitation, IV access, blood products as appropriate, vasoactive therapy and antibiotics in suspected variceal bleeding.
Q3What bedside feature suggests significant portal hypertension?⌄
Answer: Splenomegaly with collateral abdominal veins and ascites.

Case 6: Painless Progressive Jaundice with a Palpable Gallbladder

Clinical findings

A 66-year-old man has progressive jaundice, dark urine, pale stools and weight loss. There is no fever or abdominal pain. The gallbladder is palpable and non-tender.

Q1What type of jaundice is suggested?⌄
Answer: Extrahepatic obstructive jaundice.
Q2What does a palpable non-tender gallbladder suggest in this context?⌄
Answer: Distal malignant biliary obstruction, classically pancreatic head or distal bile duct malignancy.
Q3What is the classic bedside principle called?⌄
Answer: Courvoisier sign/law.

Case 7: Fever, Jaundice and Right Upper Quadrant Pain

Clinical findings

A 57-year-old woman presents with fever, jaundice and right upper quadrant pain. She is tachycardic and tender in the right hypochondrium.

Q1What acute biliary syndrome is suggested?⌄
Answer: Acute ascending cholangitis.
Q2What is the classic triad?⌄
Answer: Charcot triad: fever, jaundice and right upper quadrant pain.
Q3Which additional findings indicate severe disease?⌄
Answer: Hypotension and altered mental status, completing Reynolds pentad.

Case 8: Severe Epigastric Pain Radiating to the Back

Clinical findings

A 45-year-old man develops severe constant epigastric pain radiating to the back with repeated vomiting. He prefers sitting forward. The abdomen is tender in the epigastrium with reduced bowel sounds.

Q1What acute pancreatic process should be suspected?⌄
Answer: Acute pancreatitis.
Q2Which two common causes should be considered first?⌄
Answer: Gallstones and alcohol; hypertriglyceridaemia, drugs and other causes are also important.
Q3What bedside signs suggest severe disease?⌄
Answer: Hypotension, tachypnoea, hypoxaemia, oliguria, altered sensorium or signs of systemic inflammatory response.

Case 9: Chronic Epigastric Pain, Weight Loss and Steatorrhoea

Clinical findings

A 48-year-old man with long-term alcohol use has recurrent epigastric pain, weight loss and bulky, greasy stools. He has developed diabetes over the past year.

Q1What pancreatic syndrome is suggested?⌄
Answer: Chronic pancreatitis with exocrine and endocrine pancreatic insufficiency.
Q2What explains the greasy stools?⌄
Answer: Fat malabsorption due to inadequate pancreatic enzyme secretion.
Q3Which imaging finding would strongly support the diagnosis?⌄
Answer: Pancreatic calcification, ductal dilatation or structural changes on CT/MRCP/EUS.

Case 10: Dysphagia First for Solids, Then Liquids

Clinical findings

A 63-year-old man has progressive dysphagia for 4 months, initially for solids and later for liquids. He has lost 8 kg. There is no neurological deficit.

Q1Does the pattern suggest mechanical obstruction or motility disorder?⌄
Answer: Mechanical obstruction is more likely because dysphagia began with solids and then progressed to liquids.
Q2What diagnosis must be excluded urgently in this age group?⌄
Answer: Oesophageal malignancy.
Q3What is the next key investigation?⌄
Answer: Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy with biopsy of any suspicious lesion.

Case 11: Dysphagia for Both Solids and Liquids from Onset

Clinical findings

A 34-year-old woman has intermittent dysphagia for both solids and liquids from the beginning, regurgitation of undigested food and nocturnal cough. Weight loss is mild.

Q1Does this pattern suggest obstruction or dysmotility?⌄
Answer: Oesophageal motility disorder.
Q2What condition is classically associated with this presentation?⌄
Answer: Achalasia cardia.
Q3Which test is the physiological gold standard?⌄
Answer: High-resolution oesophageal manometry.

Case 12: Chronic Diarrhoea with Weight Loss and Nutritional Deficiency

Clinical findings

A 29-year-old woman has chronic loose stools, abdominal bloating and weight loss. She is pale, has angular stomatitis and mild pedal oedema. There is no blood in stool.

Q1What broad syndrome is present?⌄
Answer: Malabsorption.
Q2Which common immune-mediated small bowel disorder should be considered?⌄
Answer: Coeliac disease.
Q3Which initial tests are appropriate if she is consuming gluten?⌄
Answer: IgA tissue transglutaminase antibody with total IgA; endoscopic duodenal biopsy is used for confirmation when indicated.

Case 13: Bloody Diarrhoea with Tenesmus

Clinical findings

A 31-year-old man has recurrent episodes of bloody diarrhoea, urgency and tenesmus for 6 months. He has mild lower abdominal tenderness. There are no perianal fistulae.

Q1Which part of the bowel is likely involved?⌄
Answer: The colon and rectum.
Q2Which inflammatory bowel disease phenotype is more likely?⌄
Answer: Ulcerative colitis, especially with continuous distal symptoms such as bleeding, urgency and tenesmus.
Q3What serious complication should be suspected if abdominal distension, fever and systemic toxicity develop?⌄
Answer: Toxic megacolon.

Case 14: Abdominal Pain, Weight Loss and Perianal Disease

Clinical findings

A 27-year-old has chronic abdominal pain, non-bloody diarrhoea and weight loss. Examination shows perianal skin tags and a discharging fistula. There is mild right lower quadrant tenderness.

Q1Which inflammatory bowel disease phenotype is favoured?⌄
Answer: Crohn disease.
Q2Which features favour it over ulcerative colitis?⌄
Answer: Perianal fistulae, transmural complications, skip lesions and possible small-bowel involvement.
Q3What nutritional deficiency is especially common with terminal ileal disease?⌄
Answer: Vitamin B12 deficiency; bile salt malabsorption may also occur.

Case 15: Sudden Severe Abdominal Pain with a Rigid Abdomen

Clinical findings

A 50-year-old man with a history of episodic epigastric pain develops sudden severe generalized abdominal pain. He lies still. The abdomen is board-like rigid with absent bowel sounds.

Q1What acute abdominal catastrophe is suggested?⌄
Answer: Perforation of a hollow viscus with generalized peritonitis.
Q2What historical diagnosis commonly precedes this presentation?⌄
Answer: Peptic ulcer disease is a classic cause, although other perforations are possible.
Q3What simple imaging finding may support the diagnosis?⌄
Answer: Free subdiaphragmatic air on erect chest or abdominal radiography; CT is more sensitive.

Case 16: Chronic Epigastric Pain Relieved by Food

Clinical findings

A 38-year-old man has burning epigastric pain that improves after meals but recurs a few hours later and sometimes wakes him at night. There is mild epigastric tenderness.

Q1Which peptic ulcer pattern is classically suggested?⌄
Answer: Duodenal ulcer disease.
Q2What two major etiological factors should be considered?⌄
Answer: Helicobacter pylori infection and NSAID use.
Q3Which complication would cause sudden severe generalized abdominal pain?⌄
Answer: Perforation.

Case 17: Weight Loss, Early Satiety and a Left Supraclavicular Node

Clinical findings

A 64-year-old man has anorexia, early satiety and weight loss. He is pale. A hard left supraclavicular lymph node is palpable. There is mild epigastric fullness.

Q1What does the left supraclavicular node suggest?⌄
Answer: Metastatic abdominal malignancy via the thoracic duct, classically gastric carcinoma.
Q2What is this node commonly called?⌄
Answer: Virchow node.
Q3What investigation is required for diagnosis?⌄
Answer: Upper GI endoscopy with biopsy.

Case 18: Right Lower Quadrant Mass with Anaemia and Weight Loss

Clinical findings

A 67-year-old man has fatigue, weight loss and altered bowel habits. He is pale. A firm, non-tender mass is palpable in the right lower quadrant.

Q1What colonic pathology should be considered?⌄
Answer: Right-sided colonic malignancy, especially caecal or ascending colon carcinoma.
Q2Why may overt obstruction occur late in right-sided lesions?⌄
Answer: The right colon has a wider lumen and liquid contents, so tumours can grow large before causing obstruction.
Q3What laboratory abnormality is commonly found?⌄
Answer: Iron deficiency anaemia from chronic occult blood loss.

Case 19: Massive Splenomegaly with Minimal Jaundice

Clinical findings

A 32-year-old man has abdominal fullness and recurrent episodes of haematemesis. Examination shows massive splenomegaly and dilated abdominal wall veins. The liver is not enlarged and there are few stigmata of chronic liver disease.

Q1What haemodynamic syndrome is present?⌄
Answer: Portal hypertension.
Q2Does the examination favour cirrhosis or a non-cirrhotic portal hypertensive state?⌄
Answer: A non-cirrhotic portal hypertensive disorder is favoured by massive splenomegaly, preserved liver size and minimal stigmata of chronic liver disease.
Q3What important causes should be considered?⌄
Answer: Extrahepatic portal vein obstruction and non-cirrhotic portal fibrosis/porto-sinusoidal vascular disease, depending on age and setting.

Case 20: Enlarged Nodular Liver with Weight Loss

Clinical findings

A 59-year-old man with chronic liver disease develops worsening right upper quadrant discomfort and weight loss. The liver is enlarged, hard and nodular. Ascites is present.

Q1What complication should be suspected?⌄
Answer: Hepatocellular carcinoma developing in chronic liver disease.
Q2Which clinical changes should raise suspicion in a previously stable cirrhotic patient?⌄
Answer: New weight loss, worsening pain, rapidly increasing ascites, new hepatomegaly or sudden deterioration in liver function.
Q3Which investigations are appropriate for confirmation?⌄
Answer: Multiphasic contrast-enhanced CT or MRI of the liver, with AFP as a supportive but not definitive marker.
