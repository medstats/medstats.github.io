Clinical Case Summary & Differential Diagnosis (History Phase)

Case Summary (Positive Findings):

  • Demographics: 41-year-old male, Head Constable.
  • Constitutional Symptoms: Significant weight loss (5-6 kg over 6 months), nausea (1 month).
  • Gastrointestinal Symptoms: Abdominal distension (15 days, acute onset, gradually progressive, abdominal girth 96.5 cm), mild associated abdominal pain.
  • Hepatobiliary Symptoms: New-onset jaundice (noticed on day of admission, non-itchy).
  • Relevant Negatives (by history): No fever, no vomiting, no definite melena, no significant prior medical history (e.g., similar illness, TB, DM, HTN, previous jaundice).

Probability-Based Differential Diagnoses (on the basis of History):

  1. Malignancy (e.g., Pancreatic, Gastric, Colorectal, Primary Peritoneal, Hepatocellular Carcinoma) with peritoneal carcinomatosis and/or liver metastases/biliary obstruction (60%): Rationale: The combination of significant weight loss (5-6 kg over 6 months), progressive abdominal distension (highly suggestive of ascites), new-onset jaundice, and nausea in a middle-aged man strongly points towards an underlying malignancy. This presentation is classic for advanced cancer with peritoneal spread causing ascites and/or liver involvement/biliary obstruction causing jaundice. The acute onset of distension and jaundice on a background of chronic weight loss is particularly suspicious.
  2. Decompensated Cirrhosis (e.g., cryptogenic, viral, autoimmune, alcoholic - though no alcohol history) (20%): Rationale: Cirrhosis can lead to portal hypertension, causing ascites, and liver failure, causing jaundice. Weight loss can be a feature of chronic liver disease. However, the acute onset of jaundice and distension, along with significant weight loss *prior* to these acute symptoms, makes malignancy slightly more probable as the primary driver, but it remains a strong contender.
  3. Abdominal Tuberculosis with ascites and liver involvement (10%): Rationale: Abdominal tuberculosis is endemic in the region and can present with constitutional symptoms like weight loss, chronic abdominal pain, ascites, and occasionally jaundice due to liver involvement (e.g., granulomas, cholestasis) or biliary obstruction. The absence of fever is not exclusionary for TB.
  4. Chronic Pancreatitis with complications (e.g., pancreatic ascites, biliary obstruction, pseudocyst) (5%): Rationale: Chronic pancreatitis can cause weight loss and abdominal pain. If complicated by pancreatic ascites or biliary obstruction, it can lead to abdominal distension and jaundice. However, the mild nature of the abdominal pain makes it less typical for severe pancreatitis, and the overall picture leans more towards malignancy or cirrhosis.
  5. Budd-Chiari Syndrome (5%): Rationale: Hepatic venous outflow obstruction can cause ascites, hepatomegaly, and liver dysfunction leading to jaundice. Weight loss can occur with chronic illness. While it can present acutely, the overall picture with prominent weight loss and new-onset jaundice in this context makes it less likely than malignancy or cirrhosis but still a possibility to consider.

Q3 In any patient presenting with generalized abdominal distension, what are the primary differential diagnoses, often memorized as the "8 Fs"?
MODEL_ANS3 The "8 Fs" of generalized abdominal distension are Fat (obesity), Fluid (ascites), Flatus (intestinal gas/obstruction), Feces (constipation/megacolon), Fetus (pregnancy), Full bladder, Fatal tumor (abdominal/ovarian malignancy), and Fibroid.

Q4 There was a confusing handwritten note regarding melena, which you correctly queried. Clinically, how is melena defined, and what is the minimum volume of blood required to produce it?
MODEL_ANS4 Melena is defined as the passage of black, tarry stools resulting from altered blood. A minimum of about 60 mL of blood in the upper gastrointestinal tract is required to produce melena, and it must remain in the gut for at least 8 hours to be degraded.

Q5 The patient has a significant weight loss of 5–6 kg over the last 6 months along with ascites. Which specific etiologies of ascites should you strongly suspect with prominent weight loss?
MODEL_ANS5 Loss of weight in a patient with ascites prominently points towards tuberculosis, intra-abdominal malignancy, or advanced cirrhosis.

Q6 You accurately documented the absence of itching (pruritus). How does the presence or absence of pruritus help differentiate the physiological types of jaundice?
MODEL_ANS6 Pruritus is characteristically a prominent feature in obstructive (cholestatic) jaundice, whereas it is present but not as prominent in hepato-cellular jaundice, and is typically absent or very rare in haemolytic jaundice.

Q7 The patient is 41 years old. How does the age of the patient help guide your etiological diagnosis of jaundice?
MODEL_ANS7 A young person with jaundice is more likely to have viral hepatitis, whereas an elderly person with jaundice and weight loss is more likely to suffer from carcinoma of the hepatobiliary tract.

Q8 Jaundice was only noticed on the day of admission. What are the caveats of attempting to detect jaundice at night or under artificial light?
MODEL_ANS8 Jaundice should always be evaluated in broad daylight. Under artificial light, mild jaundice may be completely missed, a severely anaemic patient may appear to have mild jaundice, and fluorescent light can cast a yellow reflection in a normal eye, falsely mimicking jaundice.

Q9 In evaluating a new-onset jaundice, what specific historical points regarding potential parenteral or infectious exposures must be rigorously elicited?
MODEL_ANS9 It is imperative to ask about a history of contact with a jaundiced patient (Hepatitis A), recent travel, history of tattooing, intravenous drug abuse, blood transfusions, or high-risk sexual behavior to rule out viral hepatitis transmission (Hepatitis B or C).

Q10 You explicitly documented the absence of significant abdominal pain. What does painless jaundice generally indicate compared to painful jaundice?
MODEL_ANS10 Painless jaundice strongly suggests malignant biliary obstruction or hepato-cellular etiology, whereas fever, rigors, and right hypochondrial tenderness typically suggest choledocholithiasis, cholangitis, or acute hepatitis.

Q11 The patient complains of nausea for 1 month preceding the jaundice. What constitutional or prodromal symptoms characteristically precede the appearance of frank jaundice in acute viral hepatitis?
MODEL_ANS11 The prodrome of acute viral hepatitis classically includes profound anorexia, nausea, occasional vomiting, fatigue, and fever with chills or rigor before the appearance of jaundice.

Q12 You noted adequate urine output, but did not describe its color. How do urine and stool colors differentiate the major categories of jaundice?
MODEL_ANS12 In hepato-cellular and obstructive jaundice, the urine is high-colored/dark yellow due to conjugated bilirubin. Obstructive jaundice presents with pale or clay-colored stools, whereas stool is high-colored in haemolytic jaundice. In haemolytic jaundice, freshly passed urine is normal colored (acholuric) but darkens upon standing due to urobilinogen oxidation.

Q13 This patient has ascites but explicitly lacks pedal edema. What does this specific chronology (ascites without dependent edema) suggest regarding the pathophysiology?
MODEL_ANS13 In advanced cirrhosis with hepatocellular failure, hypoalbuminemia usually causes edema out of proportion to, or appearing concurrently with, ascites. Ascites preceding or occurring without edema can point towards peritoneal etiologies such as tuberculous or malignant peritonitis, or early portal hypertension where oncotic pressure is somewhat preserved.

Q14 You correctly asked about hematemesis and melena in the negative history. Why is a careful history of GI bleeding essential in any patient presenting with ascites?
MODEL_ANS14 A history of haematemesis or melaena in a patient with ascites is a classic indicator of cirrhosis of the liver complicated by portal hypertension and bleeding esophageal varices.

MODEL_ANS15 Prolonged diarrhoea can indicate a protein-losing enteropathy, alteration of bowel habits suggests large gut malignancy, and alternating diarrhoea and constipation is highly characteristic of intestinal tuberculosis.

PHASE 2 — PHYSICAL EXAMINATION PRESENTATION

Q1 You have written “ascites present.” Which bedside signs must you actually demonstrate before making that statement?

MODEL_ANS1 Ascites should be demonstrated by shifting dullness and/or fluid thrill, depending on the volume of fluid. Alagappan states that shifting dullness generally requires about 1000 mL, while fluid thrill is usually appreciable with a large amount of fluid under tension, >2000 mL. Puddle sign can detect smaller quantities. Therefore, the handwritten values of 500 mL and 1500 mL should not be quoted as Alagappan's thresholds. Reference: Alagappan, Ch. 6 “Abdomen”—Percussion/Ascites.

Q2 How would you clinically grade ascites?

MODEL_ANS2 Alagappan grades ascites as + detectable only by careful examination; ++ easily detectable but relatively small volume; +++ obvious but not tense; ++++ tense ascites. A statement merely saying “ascites present” is therefore incomplete. Reference: Alagappan, Ch. 6—Percussion/Grading of Ascites.

Q3 Are “flanks free” and an inverted umbilicus entirely consistent with obvious ascites?

MODEL_ANS3 They make the documentation internally less convincing. In moderate ascites, flank dullness/fullness is expected clinically, whereas Alagappan notes that cirrhosis with ascites may produce a horizontal slit-like umbilicus. Thus, if ascites is claimed, it should be objectively demonstrated by percussion rather than inferred from distension alone. Reference: Alagappan, Ch. 6—Inspection and Percussion of Abdomen.

Q4 You found dilated abdominal veins. What two additional observations are essential?

MODEL_ANS4 Document their distribution and determine the direction of blood flow. Periumbilical dilated veins or caput medusae suggest portal hypertension. Alagappan describes flow away from the umbilicus in portal hypertension, whereas prominent collateral flow in IVC obstruction is cephalad. Merely recording “dilated veins present” is incomplete. Reference: Alagappan, Ch. 6—Inspection of the Abdomen.

Q5 What is the significance of the spider naevi in this patient, and how should you document them?

MODEL_ANS5 Spider naevi should be described by number and distribution. They consist of a central arteriole with radiating vessels and usually occur in the SVC territory. Alagappan states that more than five is abnormal and indicates liver-cell failure, although small numbers may occur in healthy individuals or pregnancy. Reference: Alagappan, Ch. 6—Signs of Liver Cell Failure.

Q6 What is the likely significance of the “white nails” recorded here?

MODEL_ANS6 Alagappan describes leuconychia as occurring in hypoalbuminaemia. In a patient with suspected chronic liver disease, this may therefore support impaired hepatic synthetic function, although serum albumin would be required for confirmation. Reference: Alagappan, Ch. 6—General Examination.

Q7 How should every palpable liver be systematically described?

MODEL_ANS7 Describe the extent below the costal margin, edge, surface, consistency, tenderness, movement with respiration and pulsatility, and confirm enlargement by percussion/liver span. Your description includes most palpatory characteristics, but the liver span was not documented, leaving the assessment of true hepatomegaly incomplete. Reference: Alagappan, Ch. 6—Palpation of Liver.

Q8 What does a firm, smooth/regular palpable liver suggest?

MODEL_ANS8 According to Alagappan, a firm regular liver may occur in obstructive jaundice, cirrhosis of liver and chronic congestive cardiac failure. A nodular liver would be more suggestive of advanced secondary malignancy or hepatoma. Therefore, the present liver characteristics alone do not establish cirrhosis or malignancy. Reference: Alagappan, Ch. 6—Palpation of Liver.

Q9 What are the important differential diagnoses of painful or tender hepatomegaly?

MODEL_ANS9 Alagappan lists congestive cardiac failure, viral hepatitis, hepatic amoebiasis, pyemic abscess, hepatoma, actinomycosis, hepatic secondaries and Budd–Chiari syndrome among causes of painful hepatomegaly. Thus, tenderness is diagnostically important and should prevent premature attribution of this liver solely to uncomplicated cirrhosis. Reference: Alagappan, Ch. 6—Causes of Painful Hepatomegaly.

Q10 Does absence of palpable splenomegaly exclude cirrhosis or portal hypertension?

MODEL_ANS10 No. Alagappan notes that mild-to-moderate splenomegaly may occur in cirrhosis, but its absence does not by itself exclude cirrhosis or portal hypertension. The diagnosis must depend on the complete constellation of clinical findings. Reference: Alagappan, Ch. 6—Cirrhosis of Liver/Causes of Splenomegaly.

Q11 How would you interpret visible epigastric pulsations?

MODEL_ANS11 They should not automatically be interpreted as a manifestation of liver disease. Alagappan notes that abdominal aortic pulsations may be visible in thin individuals or in aortic aneurysm. The examiner should determine whether the pulsation is simply transmitted/aortic and correlate it with palpation. Reference: Alagappan, Ch. 6—Inspection, Visible Pulsations.

Q12 What is the importance of looking for a venous hum in this case?

MODEL_ANS12 An abdominal venous hum between the xiphisternum and umbilicus may result from turbulent flow through well-developed collaterals in portal hypertension and is associated with the Cruveilhier–Baumgarten phenomenon. Its absence, however, does not rule out portal hypertension. Reference: Alagappan, Ch. 6—Auscultation/Venous Hum.

Q13 How do the normal JVP and absence of pedal oedema influence your interpretation of the tender hepatomegaly?

MODEL_ANS13 They make systemic venous congestion from overt right-sided cardiac failure less strongly supported clinically. This is relevant because congestive cardiac failure is an important cause of tender hepatomegaly. The normal cardiovascular examination therefore helps refine, but does not independently establish, the hepatic diagnosis. Reference: Alagappan, Ch. 6—Causes of Painful Hepatomegaly.

Q14 What does absence of flapping tremor tell you?

MODEL_ANS14 Asterixis or flapping tremor is one of the bedside manifestations associated with hepatic failure. Its absence, together with a conscious and oriented state, means there is no overt clinical evidence of hepatic encephalopathy on the examination presented; it does not by itself exclude subtle encephalopathy. Reference: Alagappan, Ch. 6—Signs of Liver Cell Failure.

Q15 Before investigations, how should you summarize the examination syndrome without overcommitting to an etiological diagnosis?

MODEL_ANS15 The examination demonstrates pallor, icterus, stigmata suggestive of chronic hepatocellular dysfunction, dilated abdominal veins and tender firm smooth hepatomegaly, with ascites reported but not adequately demonstrated or graded in the findings supplied. The safest clinical formulation is therefore a hepatobiliary disorder with hepatomegaly and features suggestive of chronic liver dysfunction/portal hypertension, with possible ascites requiring bedside confirmation. PHASE 2 — PHYSICAL EXAMINATION PRESENTATION

Q1 You documented that the patient has ascites and the liver is palpable 6–7 cm below the costal margin. What specific bedside method of palpation is mandatory when examining for an enlarged liver in the presence of ascites, and how is it executed?
MODEL_ANS1 In the presence of ascites, the dipping method (dipping palpation) must always be employed to palpate the liver or spleen. The examiner places the fingers flat on the abdominal wall and depresses them with a quick, flexing movement at the metacarpophalangeal joints, momentarily displacing the fluid layer so the fingertips strike the underlying visceral surface. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Part I, Short Cases: Ascites / Abdomen).

Q2 You found a liver that is firm, smooth, and tender, palpable 6–7 cm below the costal margin. How does liver consistency clinically differentiate cirrhosis, congestive hepatomegaly, and liver malignancy?
MODEL_ANS2 Consistency is classically compared to facial landmarks: a soft liver (feels like lips) occurs in normal health, acute hepatitis, or early congestion; a firm liver (feels like the tip of the nose) is characteristic of cirrhosis, chronic passive congestion, or amyloidosis; a hard or "rock-hard" liver (feels like the forehead) typically indicates hepatocellular carcinoma, secondary metastases, or advanced chronic myeloid leukemia. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Part I, Short Cases: Liver).

Q3 What are the primary pathophysiological mechanisms responsible for hepatic tenderness, and what are the major differential diagnoses of an enlarged and tender liver?
MODEL_ANS3 Hepatic pain and tenderness arise from sudden stretching of Glisson’s capsule or inflammation of the capsule/peritoneum (perihepatitis). The major causes of an enlarged and tender liver include acute viral or alcoholic hepatitis, acute congestive cardiac failure (CCF), pyogenic or amoebic liver abscess, acute Budd-Chiari syndrome, and rapidly enlarging hepatocellular carcinoma or liver metastases. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Part I, Short Cases: Liver; Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Abdomen).

Q4 In inspection of the abdomen, you noted visible epigastric pulsations. What are the common differential diagnoses for pulsations visible or palpable in the epigastrium?
MODEL_ANS4 Epigastric pulsations may be physiological (normal aortic pulsation seen in a thin, relaxed person), due to right ventricular hypertrophy (RVH), an abdominal aortic aneurysm (expansile pulsation), transmitted pulsation from the aorta by a mass in front of it (such as a gastric or retroperitoneal tumor), or transmitted hepatic pulsation from tricuspid regurgitation. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, General Survey / Abdomen).

Q5 How do you clinically differentiate an expansile pulsation from a transmitted pulsation in an epigastric swelling or mass?
MODEL_ANS5 Clinically, the "two-finger test" is performed: the examiner places two index fingers on opposite lateral margins of the pulsation/mass. If the fingers are both pushed upwards and driven apart, the pulsation is expansile (true arterial aneurysm). If the fingers are only lifted upwards without separating, or if the pulsation disappears in the knee-elbow position (as the mass falls away from the abdominal aorta), it is a transmitted pulsation. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Part I, Short Cases: Palpation of Abdomen).

Q6 You documented visible dilated veins over the abdomen. How do you determine the direction of blood flow in dilated superficial abdominal veins using the two-finger stripping test?
MODEL_ANS6 A vein segment without visible tributaries is chosen below the umbilicus. Place two adjacent index fingers firmly over the vein, press down, and slide them apart to empty the intervening segment. Release one finger while keeping the other pressed, and observe the speed and direction of filling. Repeat the process by releasing the other finger. The vein refills faster from the true direction of venous flow. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Spot Cases: Abdominal Veins; Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Abdomen).

Q7 How does the direction of blood flow in abdominal collaterals distinguish portal hypertension from inferior vena cava (IVC) obstruction?
MODEL_ANS7 In portal hypertension, collateral blood flows away from the umbilicus in all directions (upward above the umbilicus, downward below the umbilicus). In IVC obstruction, blood is diverted to the superior vena caval system, so the direction of flow is uniformly from below upwards (cephalad), whether tested above or below the umbilicus. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Spot Cases: Prominent Veins; Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Abdomen).

Q8 You noted spider naevi on the patient. Where are spider naevi characteristically distributed over the body, and what is their diagnostic cutoff for liver cell failure?
MODEL_ANS8 Spider naevi are characteristically distributed exclusively in the territory of the superior vena cava—namely, the face, neck, upper chest, upper back, arms, and forearms. Finding more than five spider naevi is considered pathological and strongly indicates hepatocellular failure/cirrhosis. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Spot Cases: Spider Naevi; Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, General Survey / Abdomen).

Q9 What bedside test confirms that a cutaneous vascular lesion is indeed a spider nevus rather than a cherry angioma (Campbell de Morgan spot) or venous star?
MODEL_ANS9 The "blanching response" confirms a spider nevus: when pressure is applied with the head of a pin or a glass slide to the central raised arteriole, the radiating spider "legs" (capillaries) blanch completely, and upon release, rapid refilling of the capillaries from the center outwards is observed. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Part I, Spot Cases; Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine).

Q10 You observed "white nails" (leukonychia). What is the underlying pathophysiological cause of Terry’s nails in chronic liver disease?
MODEL_ANS10 White nails (Terry’s nails or leukonychia) in chronic liver disease result from hypoalbuminemia (causing nail-bed edema and an opaque, ground-glass appearance of the proximal nail bed), with preservation of a thin, pink/brown transverse band at the distal edge. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Spot Cases: Leukonychia; Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Nails).

Q11 The student notes state: Shifting dullness requires “at least 500 mL” and fluid thrill requires “around 1500 mL”. What are the standard clinical fluid volume thresholds taught for eliciting shifting dullness versus fluid thrill?
MODEL_ANS11 Shifting dullness is the most reliable clinical sign for ascites and generally requires at least 500 mL to 1000 mL of free peritoneal fluid to be elicited. A fluid thrill requires a much larger collection, classically 1500 mL to 2000 mL (1.5 to 2 liters) of fluid under tension (tense ascites). (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Short Cases: Ascites; Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Abdomen).

Q12 How does percussion of the abdomen differentiate ascites from alternative causes of abdominal distension, such as a large ovarian cyst or gaseous distension?
MODEL_ANS12 In ascites, fluid collects in the dependent parts: the flanks and hypogastrium are dull, while the central umbilical region is tympanitic (horseshoe-shaped dullness). In a large ovarian cyst or distended bladder, the midline/central area is dull, whereas the flanks remain tympanitic. In gaseous intestinal distension, both the midline and flanks are uniformly tympanitic. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Short Cases: Ascites; Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Abdomen).

Q13 You omitted the liver span and upper border of liver dullness. How is the liver span measured clinically, and what is its normal range?
MODEL_ANS13 Liver span is the vertical distance between the uppermost border of hepatic dullness (determined by heavy percussion along the right midclavicular line from resonance to dullness, normally at the 5th intercostal space) and the lowermost border (determined by light percussion or palpation from the right iliac fossa upwards). The normal liver span in an adult male along the right midclavicular line is 12 to 15 cm. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Short Cases: Liver; Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Abdomen).

Q14 The spleen was documented as "not palpable." Why can splenic enlargement sometimes be missed in patients with moderate to marked ascites, and how should it be sought?
MODEL_ANS14 In the presence of ascites, the accumulation of fluid in the left hypochondrium pushes the examiner's fingers away from the visceral surface, obscuring mild or moderate splenomegaly. Splenomegaly must be examined using the dipping technique, starting from the right iliac fossa, or by placing the patient in the right lateral decubitus position (45 degrees) with flexion of the knees to allow the spleen to fall forwards and medially. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Short Cases: Splenomegaly; Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Abdomen).

Q15 You recorded an afebrile patient with right upper quadrant tenderness, firm tender hepatomegaly, and ascites. What bedside procedure is mandatory to evaluate this ascites, and what key parameter differentiates portal hypertension from other causes?
MODEL_ANS15 A diagnostic paracentesis (ascitic tap) is mandatory. Measuring the Serum-Ascites Albumin Gradient (SAAG = serum albumin minus ascitic fluid albumin) is key: a high gradient (SAAG ≥ 1.1 g/dL) indicates portal hypertension (e.g., cirrhosis, alcoholic hepatitis, Budd-Chiari), whereas a low gradient (SAAG < 1.1 g/dL) points towards non-portal causes (peritoneal tuberculosis, peritoneal carcinomatosis, or nephrotic syndrome). (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Short Cases: Ascites / Table 2.3; Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Abdomen).
