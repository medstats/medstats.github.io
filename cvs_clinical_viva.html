Case 36: Neck Pulsations, Pulsatile Liver and a Pansystolic Murmur

Clinical findings

JVP is elevated. There are large systolic waves in the neck. Liver is enlarged and pulsatile. A pansystolic murmur is heard at the lower left sternal border and increases on inspiration.

Q1Localise the lesion.⌄
Answer: Right-sided valvular lesion, most likely tricuspid regurgitation.
Q2Explain the JVP abnormality.⌄
Answer: During right ventricular systole, blood regurgitates into the right atrium, producing prominent systolic V waves.
Q3Give three common causes.⌄
Answer: Functional tricuspid regurgitation due to pulmonary hypertension or RV dilatation; infective endocarditis; rheumatic valve disease. Other possibilities include carcinoid disease and device-related tricuspid regurgitation.

Case 37: Intermittent Large Neck Pulsations with Marked Bradycardia

Clinical findings

Intermittent very large neck pulsations are seen. They are not present with every cardiac cycle. Pulse is slow and regular at 38/min.

Q1What is this JVP finding?⌄
Answer: Cannon A waves.
Q2What is the mechanism?⌄
Answer: The right atrium contracts against a closed tricuspid valve because atrial and ventricular contractions are dissociated.
Q3What is the most likely rhythm disturbance here?⌄
Answer: Complete heart block. Other causes include ventricular tachycardia and other forms of AV dissociation.

Case 38: Low-Volume Slow-Rising Pulse with an Ejection Systolic Murmur

Clinical findings

Pulse is low-volume and slow-rising. Pulse pressure is narrow. Apex is sustained. A harsh systolic murmur is heard in the right second intercostal space and radiates to the carotids.

Q1What valve lesion does this suggest?⌄
Answer: Aortic stenosis.
Q2Which bedside findings suggest severe disease?⌄
Answer: Delayed carotid upstroke, narrow pulse pressure, late-peaking systolic murmur, and a soft or absent A2.
Q3If the murmur is unexpectedly soft despite severe symptoms, what possibility should you consider?⌄
Answer: Low-flow, low-gradient severe aortic stenosis due to reduced forward stroke volume.

Case 39: Exertional Presyncope with a Dynamic Systolic Murmur

Clinical findings

A 24-year-old has exertional presyncope. Pulse is brisk. Apex is forceful. There is an ejection systolic murmur at the left sternal border. The murmur becomes louder on standing.

Q1What is the likely mechanism of obstruction?⌄
Answer: Dynamic left ventricular outflow tract obstruction.
Q2What diagnosis should you think of?⌄
Answer: Hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy.
Q3What happens to the murmur with squatting, and why?⌄
Answer: It becomes softer because increased preload and afterload enlarge the LV cavity and reduce dynamic obstruction.

Case 40: Bounding Pulse, Wide Pulse Pressure and an Early Diastolic Murmur

Clinical findings

Pulse is bounding and rapidly collapsing. BP is 168/48 mmHg. Apex is displaced down and out. A high-pitched early diastolic murmur is heard along the left sternal edge.

Q1What is the lesion?⌄
Answer: Aortic regurgitation.
Q2Why is the pulse pressure wide?⌄
Answer: Increased stroke volume raises systolic pressure, while diastolic runoff back into the LV lowers diastolic pressure.
Q3Name two conditions that can mimic the pulse without this valve lesion.⌄
Answer: Examples include thyrotoxicosis, severe anaemia, arteriovenous fistula, pregnancy, and sepsis.

Case 41: Irregular Pulse with an Apical–Radial Rate Difference

Clinical findings

Radial pulse is 92/min and completely irregular. Apical rate is 124/min. First heart sound varies in intensity.

Q1What is the rhythm?⌄
Answer: Atrial fibrillation.
Q2What is the difference between apical and radial rates called?⌄
Answer: Pulse deficit.
Q3Why does it occur?⌄
Answer: Some ventricular contractions have inadequate filling and stroke volume, so they do not generate a palpable peripheral pulse.

Case 42: Tapping Apex, Opening Snap and Loud P2

Clinical findings

Apex is tapping and not displaced. S1 is loud. An opening snap is followed by a low-pitched diastolic murmur at the apex. P2 is loud and there is a parasternal heave.

Q1What is the primary lesion?⌄
Answer: Mitral stenosis.
Q2Which findings indicate a complication rather than the valve lesion itself?⌄
Answer: Loud P2 and parasternal heave indicate pulmonary hypertension and right ventricular pressure overload.
Q3What change in heart sounds can occur if the valve becomes heavily calcified?⌄
Answer: S1 and the opening snap may become softer or disappear.

Case 43: Displaced Hyperdynamic Apex with a Pansystolic Murmur

Clinical findings

Apex is displaced down and laterally and is hyperdynamic. S1 is soft. A pansystolic murmur at the apex radiates to the axilla.

Q1Which valve is abnormal?⌄
Answer: The mitral valve; the lesion is mitral regurgitation.
Q2Why is the apex hyperdynamic?⌄
Answer: The LV ejects into both the low-pressure left atrium and the aorta, producing increased total stroke volume.
Q3What bedside change would suggest an acute rather than chronic severe lesion?⌄
Answer: Severe pulmonary oedema with a non-displaced apex and possibly a relatively soft or short murmur.

Case 44: Wide Fixed Splitting of S2 in a Young Adult

Clinical findings

A young adult has a parasternal heave, wide fixed splitting of S2 and an ejection systolic murmur in the pulmonary area.

Q1What congenital lesion is most likely?⌄
Answer: Atrial septal defect.
Q2Is the pulmonary systolic murmur produced by blood flowing across the septal defect?⌄
Answer: No. It is a flow murmur caused by increased flow across the pulmonary valve.
Q3What does the parasternal heave represent?⌄
Answer: Right ventricular volume overload.

Case 45: Cyanosis, Clubbing, Loud P2 and a Previously Louder Childhood Murmur

Clinical findings

A 28-year-old has central cyanosis and clubbing. There is a parasternal heave, very loud P2 and only a soft systolic murmur. A loud murmur was documented in childhood.

Q1What physiological transition has probably occurred?⌄
Answer: A previous left-to-right shunt has developed severe pulmonary vascular disease and has become bidirectional or reversed.
Q2What syndrome is this?⌄
Answer: Eisenmenger syndrome.
Q3Why has the original murmur become softer?⌄
Answer: The pressure gradient across the defect has fallen as right ventricular and pulmonary artery pressures approach systemic levels.

Case 46: Raised JVP, Rapid y Descent, Ascites and Clear Lungs

Clinical findings

JVP is markedly raised. x and y descents are prominent. JVP rises on inspiration. There is ascites, hepatomegaly and relatively clear lungs. A high-frequency early diastolic sound is heard.

Q1What is the likely diagnosis?⌄
Answer: Constrictive pericarditis.
Q2What is the early diastolic sound?⌄
Answer: Pericardial knock.
Q3What major differential should always be considered?⌄
Answer: Restrictive cardiomyopathy.

Case 47: Hypotension, Raised JVP and a Marked Inspiratory BP Fall

Clinical findings

The patient is hypotensive and tachycardic. JVP is elevated. Heart sounds are muffled. The y descent is poorly seen. Systolic BP falls markedly on inspiration.

Q1What is the likely diagnosis?⌄
Answer: Cardiac tamponade.
Q2What is the inspiratory fall in systolic pressure called?⌄
Answer: Pulsus paradoxus.
Q3Why is the y descent blunted?⌄
Answer: High intrapericardial pressure prevents rapid right atrial emptying during early diastole.

Case 48: Prominent A Waves, RV Heave and Hyperinflated Lungs

Clinical findings

JVP has prominent A waves. There is a parasternal heave and loud P2. Lungs are hyperinflated with reduced air entry. There is no displaced LV apex.

Q1What chamber is under pressure overload?⌄
Answer: The right ventricle.
Q2What is the likely cardiovascular syndrome?⌄
Answer: Pulmonary hypertension with cor pulmonale.
Q3Why are A waves prominent?⌄
Answer: The right atrium contracts against a stiff, high-pressure right ventricle.

Case 49: Double Apical Impulse with Exertional Dizziness

Clinical findings

A young man with exertional dizziness has a forceful, bifid or double apical impulse. Carotid pulse is brisk. There is a systolic murmur at the left sternal border.

Q1What diagnosis should this combination suggest?⌄
Answer: Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.
Q2What can produce a double apical impulse?⌄
Answer: A prominent atrial contraction against a stiff hypertrophied LV followed by ventricular systole.
Q3What family-history question is particularly important?⌄
Answer: Ask about sudden unexplained death, cardiomyopathy, or premature sudden cardiac arrest in relatives.

Case 50: Loud S1 and a Low-Pitched Apical Mid-Diastolic Murmur

Clinical findings

The patient has exertional dyspnoea. S1 is loud. There is a low-pitched apical mid-diastolic murmur. No opening snap is heard. Rhythm is sinus.

Q1Does absence of an opening snap exclude the suspected lesion?⌄
Answer: No.
Q2Why may the opening snap be absent?⌄
Answer: The valve may be heavily calcified or immobile; very severe disease can also make the opening snap difficult to appreciate.
Q3What bedside feature can help distinguish true valve obstruction from increased transmitral flow?⌄
Answer: A tapping apex, loud S1, presystolic accentuation in sinus rhythm, and signs of pulmonary hypertension favour true mitral stenosis.

Case 51: Pansystolic Murmur that Becomes Louder on Inspiration

Clinical findings

A pansystolic murmur is heard near the lower left sternal edge. It becomes significantly louder during inspiration. There is no radiation to the axilla.

Q1Which side of the heart is the murmur likely arising from?⌄
Answer: The right side of the heart.
Q2What is this inspiratory augmentation principle called?⌄
Answer: Carvallo sign.
Q3Which lesion is most likely?⌄
Answer: Tricuspid regurgitation. Ventricular septal defect is an important differential but typically does not show this inspiratory augmentation.

Case 52: Loud P2 with an Upper Left Sternal Early Diastolic Murmur

Clinical findings

A patient with severe pulmonary hypertension has a loud P2 and a high-pitched early diastolic murmur at the upper left sternal border.

Q1What is this murmur called?⌄
Answer: Graham Steell murmur.
Q2What lesion produces it?⌄
Answer: Functional pulmonary regurgitation due to severe pulmonary hypertension with pulmonary artery or annular dilatation.
Q3How would you distinguish it clinically from an aortic-origin early diastolic murmur?⌄
Answer: Marked pulmonary hypertension, loud P2 and an RV heave favour pulmonary regurgitation; a bounding pulse and wide pulse pressure favour aortic regurgitation.
