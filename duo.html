STAGE 1: Initial Presentation & Baseline Workup

A 74-year-old man, a health-care worker and lifelong nonsmoker from a suburb of a North Indian metro with a background of hypertension and dyslipidemia, presents to the emergency department with dyspnea, proximal muscle weakness, and hypoxemia.

Timeline of the illness:
- 7 months before presentation: intermittent binocular diplopia developed. An ophthalmologist documented right-eye ptosis and fluctuating strabismus without a cranial nerve abnormality. Acetylcholine receptor antibodies were positive. CT chest showed a normal thymus. Pyridostigmine was started for presumed ocular myasthenia gravis.
- 5 months before presentation: diarrhea developed after starting pyridostigmine, which was stopped. Prednisone was started; the diarrhea resolved, but ocular symptoms persisted and the prednisone dose was increased gradually over 2 months.
- 3 months before presentation: ocular symptoms persisted despite the higher prednisone dose. White-cell count 8970/microliter, absolute lymphocyte count 580/microliter (reference 720-4100), platelet count 146,000/microliter. Mycophenolate mofetil was added.
- 2 months before presentation: ocular symptoms resolved, but leg swelling, weight gain, moon facies, and oral thrush developed. Prednisone was tapered.
- 5 weeks before presentation: a painful tongue lesion developed, limiting oral intake. Viscous-lidocaine mouthwash provided no relief.
- 3 weeks before presentation: two to three episodes of nonbloody, watery diarrhea per day began.
- 6 days before presentation: progressive proximal leg weakness made rising from a seated position difficult; dyspnea on exertion developed.

On presentation:
- Examination: temperature 37.2°C, BP 112/64, HR 111, RR 22, SpO2 87% on room air (rising to 98% on 5 L/min nasal cannula), BMI 24.5. Horizontal diplopia on sustained upward gaze, weakness on eye closure, 4/5 strength in elbow and hip flexion, 4/5 in shoulder abduction after 20 repetitions, hoarse voice, mild dysarthria, counted to 16 in one breath. Tongue: raised erythematous area on the dorsal aspect, ulcerated lateral lesions, and a white dorsal exudate. Mild ankle edema, no rash.
- Key labs: Hemoglobin 10.5 g/dL, hematocrit 31.5%, WBC 4520 (neutrophils 3860, lymphocytes 40, monocytes 140), platelets 129,000; AST 51, ALT 59, ALP 42, total bilirubin 0.5, albumin 2.5 g/dL. Arterial blood gas: pH 7.51, pCO2 29 mm Hg, pO2 81 mm Hg.
- CT chest (pulmonary-artery protocol): no pulmonary embolism; scattered mild interlobular septal thickening, bronchial-wall thickening, a 6-mm centrilobular nodule, bibasilar atelectasis with heterogeneity in the left lower lobe and a trace left effusion (findings possibly reflecting aspiration).
- Stool testing for C. difficile and Shiga toxins 1 and 2: negative.

Medications at admission: amlodipine, mycophenolate mofetil, omeprazole, prednisone, and prophylactic sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim. Prior screening (3 months ago) for hepatitis B/C and an interferon-gamma release assay for tuberculosis were negative. He had spent the previous 4 weeks on a coastal holiday.

Hello, Chief Resident here.

Key Findings: Profound lymphopenia (ALC 40/µL) secondary to prednisone and mycophenolate mofetil, painful ulcerative tongue lesions, chronic diarrhea, proximal weakness, hypoxemic respiratory distress, and CT evidence of subtle pulmonary infiltrates/aspiration.

STAGE 4:

Seven days after returning home, weakness recurred without bulbar or respiratory symptoms. While additional immune-globulin infusions were being planned, a syncopal event occurred and the patient returned to the emergency department.

Second admission findings:
- The tongue ulcer persisted with worsening pain and masslike swelling of the left anterior tongue. CMV DNA had fallen to 75 IU/mL (good viral suppression on valganciclovir).

Opening pressure was 24 cm of water, ranging 26-30 cm on subsequent measurements. A lumbar drain was placed to keep pressure <25 cm, later removed due to overdrainage/infection concerns, followed by daily therapeutic lumbar punctures and serial ophthalmologic exams.

Despite treatment, blood cultures remained positive for C. neoformans for 2 weeks, and CSF Gram stain remained positive 1 month after starting treatment, reflecting an extremely high organism burden. Top 15 Takeaways for Clinicians

  1. Biopsy the accessible lesion early. In iatrogenically immunosuppressed patients, an ulcerating mucosal lesion is the highest-yield diagnostic window — tissue with special stains and viral IHC beats empiricism and converts a broad differential into a treatable diagnosis.
  2. Know the "net state of immunosuppression." Combined glucocorticoid plus mycophenolate mofetil suppresses innate defenses and markedly impairs lymphocyte expansion, creating susceptibility to viral reactivation, invasive fungal disease, and mycobacterial infection.
  3. Absolute lymphopenia is a red flag. An ALC of 40/µL (reference 720–4100) signals profound T-cell depletion; every fever, mucosal ulcer, or pulmonary infiltrate in such a patient is an opportunistic infection until proven otherwise.
  4. Demand a unifying diagnosis. Destructive oral ulcers plus diarrhea, hypoxemia, cytopenias, and hepatitis in one patient is a single systemic process — resist anchoring on medication toxicity, a localized neoplasm, or a coexisting autoimmune flare.
  5. CMV reactivation is a multimodality masquerader. Disseminated CMV can simultaneously cause tissue-invasive oral ulcers, colitis, hepatitis, bone-marrow suppression, and pneumonitis — mimicking disseminated histoplasmosis or lymphoma.
  6. CMV serology distinguishes primary from reactivation. Positive CMV IgG with negative IgM and a quantitative CMV DNA of 6.27 million IU/mL indicates high-level reactivation of latent infection rather than primary acquisition.
  7. Ganciclovir myelotoxicity matters in cytopenic patients. Ganciclovir induction is standard for severe disseminated CMV, but its dose-limiting myelosuppression is a real concern in an already trilineage-cytopenic host — monitor counts and viral load closely.
  8. Transition to oral therapy when stable. Switch from intravenous ganciclovir to oral valganciclovir only after 2–3 weeks of therapy with a sustained viral decline, intact gastrointestinal absorption, and adequate renal function.
  9. CMV itself amplifies immunosuppression. Beyond direct tissue injury, CMV modulates antigen presentation and cytokine signaling and downregulates the scavenger receptors CD36 and SCARF1 on monocytes and macrophages — impairing clearance of pathogens such as Cryptococcus neoformans.
  10. Pathogens travel in packs. In profound immunosuppression, identifying one organism (CMV) must never halt the search for concurrent opportunists; a non-healing lesion despite treated viremia demands re-biopsy for a second invader.
  11. A non-healing lesion is itself a diagnostic test. The tongue ulcer that worsened despite a fall in CMV DNA (75 IU/mL) prompted re-biopsy, which revealed encapsulated budding yeast — cryptococcus — not refractory CMV.
  12. Screen for cryptococcus early. Serum cryptococcal antigen (lateral flow) plus fungal blood cultures should be part of the primary workup in any patient with severe iatrogenic immunosuppression, mucosal lesions, or neurologic symptoms — not a delayed afterthought.
  13. Treat cryptococcal meningitis in three phases. Induction with liposomal amphotericin B plus flucytosine (2 weeks), consolidation with fluconazole (8–10 weeks), then maintenance fluconazole for at least 12 months in immunocompromised hosts.
  14. Aggressively control intracranial pressure. Elevated ICP affects over half of cryptococcal meningitis cases and is a leading cause of death; use serial therapeutic lumbar punctures (target <25 cm water) and consider a lumbar drain/EVD — acetazolamide and mannitol are not recommended.
  15. High organism burden predicts death. Persistently positive blood cultures at 2 weeks and positive CSF Gram stain at 1 month reflect an overwhelming cryptococcal burden with a grim prognosis despite appropriate antifungal therapy and ICP management.
