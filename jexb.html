Clinical Case Summary & Differential Diagnosis (History Phase)

Case Summary (Positive Findings):

  • Demographics/Social: 49-year-old male, upper-lower socioeconomic status, sweeper.
  • Substance Use: Chronic heavy alcohol consumption for approximately 25 years, escalating to ~5 quarters/day of locally brewed alcohol for the last year.
  • Presenting Symptoms (Chronological):
    • Bilateral pitting pedal edema (Grade II, up to knees) for 1 month.
    • Progressive, non-itchy yellowish discoloration of sclera and skin (jaundice) for 10 days.
    • Progressive abdominal distension (girth ~114 cm, generalized, discomfort, difficulty breathing, worsened post-meals) for 8 days.
    • Progressive oliguria (~500 mL/day, dark yellow urine) for 4-5 days.
    • One episode of melena during hospital stay.
  • Associated Symptoms:
    • Decreased appetite for 1 week.
    • History of fever and loose stools 4 days prior to presentation.
  • Relevant Negative History: No previous jaundice, diabetes, hypertension, tuberculosis, significant weight loss, facial puffiness, or hematemesis (though melena occurred). Jaundice not associated with itching.

Probability-Based Differential Diagnoses (on the basis of History):

  1. Alcoholic Liver Disease (Cirrhosis with Decompensation) (70%): The extremely heavy and chronic alcohol consumption is the most striking feature. The sequential development of pedal edema (due to hypoalbuminemia/fluid retention), progressive jaundice (hepatic dysfunction), abdominal distension (ascites), and oliguria (suggesting hepatorenal syndrome or acute kidney injury secondary to liver failure) are classic manifestations of decompensated alcoholic cirrhosis. The episode of melena strongly points towards variceal bleeding from portal hypertension, a common and severe complication.
  2. Acute Alcoholic Hepatitis superimposed on Chronic Liver Disease (15%): Given the rapid progression of jaundice and ascites, especially in the context of recent fever and loose stools (which can sometimes precipitate or accompany acute alcoholic hepatitis), this represents a severe acute inflammatory exacerbation of underlying chronic alcoholic liver disease. It fits within the broader spectrum of alcoholic liver disease but highlights the acute, severe inflammatory component.
  3. Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) secondary to another cause, complicated by fluid overload and liver dysfunction (5%): While hepatorenal syndrome is likely, other causes of AKI could be considered. The history of fever and loose stools 4 days prior could have led to significant dehydration, precipitating pre-renal AKI. This AKI could then contribute to fluid overload (edema, ascites) and potentially worsen or unmask underlying liver dysfunction, or even cause secondary liver injury. However, the primary driver of jaundice and ascites appears to be liver-related.
  4. Viral Hepatitis (e.g., Hepatitis B or C) with superimposed alcohol use (5%): Chronic viral hepatitis can lead to cirrhosis and its complications (edema, jaundice, ascites, variceal bleeding, AKI). The heavy alcohol use would accelerate disease progression and worsen outcomes. Although no specific risk factors for viral hepatitis are mentioned in the history, it remains a common cause of chronic liver disease globally and should be considered in a patient presenting with decompensated liver disease.
  5. Budd-Chiari Syndrome (5%): This rare condition, caused by hepatic venous outflow obstruction, can present with rapid onset of ascites, abdominal pain, and hepatomegaly, progressing to liver failure, jaundice, and edema. The rapid development of ascites and abdominal distension, along with pedal edema, could fit. However, the prominent and progressive jaundice, and the very strong history of chronic heavy alcohol use, make other liver diseases much more probable. Melena would be a less typical initial presentation but could occur with severe portal hypertension.

]Q1 From the history alone, what is your syndromic diagnosis, and which parts should you avoid overcalling?

MODEL_ANS1 The history strongly suggests decompensated chronic liver disease, most likely alcohol-related, with ascites, jaundice, peripheral edema and probable portal-hypertensive gastrointestinal bleeding. The oliguria indicates acute renal dysfunction, but hepatorenal syndrome cannot be diagnosed from history alone; Alagappan requires cirrhosis with ascites, renal dysfunction and exclusion of shock, nephrotoxins, structural renal disease and non-response to appropriate volume expansion. Similarly, melena suggests an upper GI bleed, but a variceal source is not proven until evaluated. Alagappan, Abdomen—Cirrhosis of Liver, Variceal Bleeding, Hepatorenal Syndrome, pp. 450–461.

Q2 Your alcohol history is very important. How would you make it examination-worthy rather than simply saying “5 quarters/day”?

MODEL_ANS2 Document the type of alcohol, quantity per day, frequency, total duration, escalation in consumption, last drink, recent binge, periods of abstinence and features of dependence or withdrawal. Where possible, estimate ethanol consumption, but locally brewed alcohol cannot be converted reliably without knowing its alcohol concentration. Long-duration heavy alcohol exposure is strongly associated with alcoholic liver disease and cirrhosis. Alagappan, Cirrhosis of Liver—Alcohol-related cirrhosis.

Q3 What additional history would you ask regarding the pedal edema to distinguish hepatic edema from cardiac or renal edema?

MODEL_ANS3 Ask about facial puffiness, morning predominance, frothy urine or proteinuria symptoms for renal disease; and exertional dyspnea, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea and reduced effort tolerance for cardiac failure. Hepatic edema in cirrhosis commonly accompanies ascites and impaired albumin synthesis. Alagappan, Introduction—Oedema and Cirrhosis of Liver. Kundu, General Survey—Oedema.

Q4 What important points are missing from your jaundice history?

MODEL_ANS4 Ask specifically about dark urine, pale/clay-coloured stools, pruritus, fever, right upper abdominal pain, preceding anorexia/nausea, drug exposure and previous episodes of jaundice. These help distinguish hepatocellular from obstructive and haemolytic patterns. Your dark urine is useful, but stool colour was not documented. Alagappan, Abdomen—Jaundice, p. 437; Kundu, General Survey—Jaundice.

Q5 How should you expand the history of abdominal distension in a suspected cirrhotic patient?

MODEL_ANS5 Establish onset, rate of progression, previous similar episodes, abdominal pain, fever, early satiety, respiratory discomfort, weight change and treatment such as diuretics or paracentesis. In a patient with ascites, new fever or abdominal pain should raise concern for an infective complication such as spontaneous bacterial peritonitis. Alagappan, Ascites, p. 457; Kundu, Ascites, p. 246.

Q6 The urine output is approximately 500 mL/day. What must you ask before proposing hepatorenal syndrome?

MODEL_ANS6 Clarify baseline and measured 24-hour urine output, oral intake, vomiting/diarrhea, bleeding, fever or sepsis, recent diuretic use, NSAIDs or other nephrotoxins, hypotensive episodes and previous renal disease. Hepatorenal syndrome is a diagnosis requiring exclusion of alternative causes of renal failure rather than being inferred from oliguria alone. Alagappan, Hepatorenal Syndrome, p. 461.

Q7 How would you characterize the episode of melena adequately?

MODEL_ANS7 Ask whether the stool was black, tarry and sticky, its amount and frequency, whether there was associated hematemesis, dizziness, syncope, palpitations or worsening oliguria, and whether similar bleeding occurred previously. In cirrhosis, portal hypertension may produce oesophageal or gastric variceal bleeding, but peptic and other upper GI causes remain possible. Alagappan, Variceal Bleeding, pp. 455–456.

Q8 Which history points would you ask to screen for hepatic encephalopathy?

MODEL_ANS8 Ask relatives about sleep reversal, altered behaviour or personality, irritability, forgetfulness, confusion, inappropriate speech, drowsiness and previous episodes of altered sensorium. Hepatic encephalopathy may progress from altered sleep and mild confusion to stupor and coma. Alagappan, Hepatic Encephalopathy, p. 460.

Q9 Which precipitating factors for hepatic encephalopathy should be actively sought in this patient?

MODEL_ANS9 Important precipitants include GI bleeding, constipation, infection, dehydration or hypovolemia, electrolyte disturbance, excessive protein load, sedatives, diuretics, renal dysfunction, a large alcohol binge and large-volume paracentesis. Your patient's melena, recent fever/diarrhea and oliguria make this enquiry especially relevant. Alagappan, Hepatic Encephalopathy—Precipitating Factors.

Q10 Is “no blood transfusion” sufficient as the viral hepatitis risk history?

MODEL_ANS10 No. Ask about previous injections or invasive procedures, tattooing/piercing, intravenous drug use, high-risk sexual exposure, surgery/dental procedures, occupational blood exposure, household contact with hepatitis and previous documented hepatitis B or C infection. Kundu discusses detailed etiological history under the hepatobiliary long-case framework, while Alagappan covers viral hepatitis within the hepatology section.

Q11 Which symptoms of chronic liver cell failure or long-standing liver disease should have been asked specifically?

MODEL_ANS11 Ask for fatigue, longstanding anorexia, weight loss, muscle wasting, previous jaundice, previous ascites or edema, GI bleeding and neuropsychiatric symptoms. Alagappan lists anorexia, weight loss, muscle wasting, jaundice and fatigue among common clinical manifestations of cirrhosis, with later portal hypertension and encephalopathy. Alagappan, Cirrhosis of Liver—Clinical Features, p. 451.

Q12 Which two major non-hepatic systems must still be reviewed in a patient with edema, ascites and oliguria?

MODEL_ANS12 The cardiovascular and renal systems must be reviewed. Ask for orthopnea, PND and exertional dyspnea suggesting heart failure, and for facial puffiness, frothy urine, hematuria and prior renal disease suggesting renal pathology. This prevents premature attribution of all fluid retention to cirrhosis. Alagappan, Oedema—Aetiology and characteristic features.

Q13 What history would you take to screen for hepatocellular carcinoma in a cirrhotic patient?

MODEL_ANS13 Ask for recent unexplained weight loss, worsening anorexia, right upper quadrant pain, a newly noticed abdominal mass and unexpectedly rapid deterioration or worsening ascites. Cirrhosis is an important background condition in which hepatocellular carcinoma must be considered. Alagappan, Hepatocellular Carcinoma, p. 462.

Q14 Why is the recent fever and loose stools clinically important rather than merely an incidental past history?

MODEL_ANS14 They may represent infection and/or volume depletion, either of which can precipitate renal dysfunction in cirrhosis. Infection is also a recognised precipitating factor for hepatic encephalopathy, and potentially reversible causes must be excluded before labeling oliguria as hepatorenal syndrome. Alagappan, Hepatic Encephalopathy and Hepatorenal Syndrome.

Q15 What further personal and social history would strengthen this case presentation?

MODEL_ANS15 Document smoking/tobacco and other substance use, detailed nutritional intake, recent reduction in food intake, occupational exposures, living conditions, financial/social impact of alcohol, and dependence/withdrawal features. In a chronic alcohol user, nutritional deficiency and alcohol-related marrow suppression or GI blood loss may accompany liver disease. Alagappan, Alcoholic Liver Disease/Cirrhosis.

]Q1 You mentioned the patient has consumed "5 quarters/day of locally brewed alcohol (desi Malta)" for the last year and has been drinking for 25 years. How do you quantify alcohol intake in standard units and grams, and what is defined as the cirrhogenic or danger threshold of alcohol consumption?
MODEL_ANS1 One unit of alcohol corresponds to approximately 10 grams of absolute alcohol, which is equivalent to 30 mL of spirits/whisky, 45 mL of country liquor, 100 mL of wine, or 250 mL of beer. The danger dose of alcohol intake is considered to be >80 g/day. Cirrhosis develops at a rate 6 times greater when consumption is 40–60 g/day, and the cirrhogenic dose is classicially recognized as an average intake of 160 g/day for at least 8 years (or 180 g/day for 25 years). Consuming 5 quarters (~900 mL) of country liquor per day equals roughly 200 g of ethanol daily, far exceeding the threshold. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Long Cases / Cirrhosis of Liver; Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Substance Abuse / Alcoholic Liver Disease).

Q2 In your history, bilateral pedal edema was noted 1 month ago, preceding abdominal distension which appeared 8 days ago. What is the usual chronological sequence of fluid accumulation in cirrhosis, and what does pedal edema preceding ascites indicate?
MODEL_ANS2 In uncomplicated portal hypertension due to cirrhosis, ascites classically precedes or develops simultaneously with pedal edema. When dependent pedal edema precedes ascites, it suggests profound hypoalbuminemia (due to advanced hepatocellular failure or poor protein nutrition) lowering oncotic pressure, underlying cardiac failure, or secondary inferior vena caval (IVC) compression/functional obstruction by massive ascites. (Reference: Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Long Cases / Cirrhosis of Liver).

Q3 The patient experienced an episode of melena during the hospital stay. What is the clinical definition of melena, what is the minimum volume of blood needed to produce it, and how long must blood remain in the gastrointestinal tract?
MODEL_ANS3 Melena is defined as the passage of black, tarry stools rendered dark by the presence of altered blood. A minimum of about 60 mL of blood in the upper gastrointestinal tract is required to produce melena. The blood must remain in the gut for at least 8 hours for digestive enzymes and intestinal bacteria to degrade hemoglobin into hematin. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Abdomen / Melena).

Q4 The past history notes "fever and loose stools 4 days prior" to admission. What critical complication of decompensated cirrhosis does this suggest, and why is this history clinically significant?
MODEL_ANS4 This presentation strongly suggests Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis (SBP) or gastroenteritis. SBP can present insidiously with low-grade fever, loose motions, abdominal discomfort, or worsening ascites. Any intercurrent infection or diarrhea induces dehydration and hemodynamic stress, which commonly precipitates acute decompensation, hepatorenal syndrome (HRS), variceal bleeding, or hepatic encephalopathy. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Cirrhosis of Liver / SBP; Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Long Cases).

Q5 The patient noted a urine output of approximately 500 mL/day. How is oliguria defined, and what are the diagnostic features distinguishing Type 1 from Type 2 Hepatorenal Syndrome (HRS)?
MODEL_ANS5 Oliguria is defined as a 24-hour urine output of less than 400 mL/day (or <30 mL/hour). Hepatorenal syndrome is a functional renal failure occurring in advanced cirrhosis with structurally intact kidneys. Type 1 HRS is marked by an acute, rapidly progressive deterioration of renal function within 2 weeks, with a doubling of initial serum creatinine to >2.5 mg/dL or a 50% decrease in creatinine clearance to <20 mL/min, carrying a very poor prognosis. Type 2 HRS is characterized by a slower, steadily progressive renal insufficiency associated with refractory ascites. (Reference: Alagappan, Manual of Practical Medicine, Cirrhosis of Liver / Hepatorenal Syndrome; Kundu, Bedside Clinics in Medicine, Long Cases).

Q8 How does the clinical history of jaundice help distinguish hepatocellular jaundice from extrahepatic obstructive jaundice?
MODEL_ANS8 Hepatocellular jaundice is typically accompanied by anorexia, fatigue, malaise, and dark/high-colored urine without intense pruritus.
