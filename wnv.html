5. WNV serology caveat: IgM can persist >12 months; future febrile illnesses should not be misattributed to recurrent WNV without confirmatory PCR or seroconversion.
6. Long-term follow-up: Neurology and PM&R joint clinic at 3, 6, and 12 months to monitor motor recovery and manage spasticity that may emerge during re-innervation.
7. Public health reporting: Ensure formal notification to district surveillance unit for mosquito control interventions in the patient's residential area.

Top 15 Takeaways for Clinicians

  1. Classic Triad of WNV Neuroinvasive Disease: High fever, a non-pruritic trunk/limb morbilliform exanthem, and acute asymmetric flaccid paralysis during monsoon vector season is pathognomonic for West Nile Virus anterior horn cell myelitis.
  2. Selective Anterior Horn Cell Tropism: WNV targets the motor neurons of the anterior spinal cord, producing an Acute Flaccid Paralysis (AFP) syndrome clinically indistinguishable from poliovirus (WNV Poliomyelitis-like Syndrome).
  3. Preserved Reflexes Early in Disease: Unlike Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS) which presents with hyporeflexia or areflexia, viral anterior horn cell myelitis can present with preserved (2+) reflexes before extensive motor neuron cell death occurs.
  4. Radiologic Landmark of Viral AFM: Spine MRI characteristically demonstrates longitudinally extensive T2 hyperintensity restricted to the central gray matter (anterior horn cells / "owl's eye" sign) without contrast enhancement.
  5. Absence of Contrast Enhancement Signals Cytotoxicity: Non-enhancing anterior horn cell lesions indicate direct viral cytopathic destruction rather than post-infectious autoimmune demyelination (such as NMOSD or MOGAD).
  6. Harm of Empiric Immunosuppression in Active Viral Myelitis: Initiating high-dose corticosteroids or therapeutic plasma exchange (PLEX) in viral AFM is ineffective and potentially harmful by inhibiting viral clearance.
  7. Diagnostic Gold Standard: CSF WNV IgM antibody via ELISA is the primary diagnostic test for neuroinvasive WNV, as IgM does not cross the blood-brain barrier and indicates acute central nervous system infection.
  8. Limitations of Serum PCR and Serology: Serum WNV PCR is insensitive due to rapid transient viremia; however, serum WNV IgM is supportive and paired acute/convalescent serologies confirm diagnosis.
  9. Monsoon Vector Season Epidemiology: Arboviral neuroinvasive infections peak during monsoon/post-monsoon months (August–October) corresponding to Culex mosquito breeding cycles.
  10. Respiratory Surveillance for Diaphragmatic Weakness: Cervical cord or brainstem WNV involvement can cause diaphragmatic paralysis; serial monitoring of Forced Vital Capacity (FVC) and Negative Inspiratory Force (NIF) is essential.
  11. Management of Re-innervation Dysesthesias: As recovering motor axons re-innervate tissue, patients frequently experience painful burning neuropathic dysesthesias, which respond to gabapentinoids (pregabalin/gabapentin) or duloxetine.
  12. Long-Term Functional Prognosis: Motor recovery following WNV poliomyelitis-like paralysis is slow and often incomplete; only 30–50% of affected patients regain independent ambulation over 6–12 months.
  13. Persistence of CSF/Serum IgM Antibodies: WNV IgM antibodies can persist in serum and CSF for over 12 months, requiring cautious interpretation during subsequent febrile episodes.
  14. Public Health Reporting and Vector Control: Every confirmed or suspected case of WNV neuroinvasive disease mandates immediate notification to public health authorities for municipal mosquito control interventions.
  15. Multidisciplinary Rehabilitation Focus: Comprehensive management requires early physical, occupational, and neuro-urologic rehabilitation to manage neurogenic bladder, prevent joint contractures, and maximize functional independence.
